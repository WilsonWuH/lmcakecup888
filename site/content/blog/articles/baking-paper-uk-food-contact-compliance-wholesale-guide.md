## Introduction

Sourcing baking paper for the United Kingdom after Brexit means navigating a framework that looks familiar but no longer moves with Brussels. Great Britain operates on assimilated law — EU food-contact rules that were carried over at the end of the transition period and are now amended independently — while Northern Ireland continues to follow EU rules under the Windsor Framework. For a wholesale buyer, the practical consequence is that a compliance file assembled purely for EU customers is close to sufficient, but not identical, and retailers and auditors in the UK apply their own expectations on top of the legal baseline. This guide walks through the legal instruments that apply, the declarations and test reports UK buyers should collect, and the audit and labelling practices that keep shipments moving into British bakeries, foodservice groups and retail private-label programmes. ## The Legal Baseline: Assimilated Regulation (EC) No 1935/2004 and Related Instruments

The cornerstone for Great Britain is assimilated Regulation (EC) No 1935/2004, which retains the same core principles as the EU original: materials must not transfer constituents to food in quantities that endanger health or change composition or taste, food-contact materials must carry the designated glass-and-fork symbol or wording where packaging does not make the use obvious, and traceability must be maintained one step up and one step down the supply chain. In Northern Ireland, the EU Regulation itself continues to apply directly, so a single shipment into Belfast and Birmingham should be documented to satisfy the stricter of the two readings.

There is no UK-specific harmonised measure for paper and board comparable to the EU plastics regulation. Instead, buyers rely on the retained framework plus national regulations — such as the Materials and Articles in Contact with Food (England) Regulations 2012 and equivalents for Scotland and Wales — and on industry-recognised technical benchmarks. In practice, German BfR Recommendation XXXVI (paper and board) and the Council of Europe Resolution AP(2002)1 are the references most UK technical buyers ask for when a paper-grade specification is being verified, even though neither is binding in Britain. Treating them as de facto acceptance criteria in the purchase specification closes the gap between "legal" and "acceptable to the customer".

## Declarations of Compliance, Migration Testing and the Documents That Matter

Because Article 16 and 17 of the assimilated regulation survive in UK law, a Declaration of Compliance remains the anchor document for baking paper entering Great Britain. A sound DoC for a silicone-coated baking paper should identify the supplier and manufacturer, describe the paper construction (base grammage, bleaching route, silicone system), list applicable regulations and technical references, state the intended food types and temperature range, and be dated, versioned and signed. Batch-level traceability should link each delivered lot to the test record behind the declaration, so a retailer audit can walk from a pallet in a distribution centre back to a laboratory file.

Migration expectations for the UK market follow the same logic as for the EU: overall migration plus specific migrations relevant to the construction — for silicone-coated grades, siloxane-related parameters; for printed or film-coated variants, the ink and coating constituents. Most UK buyers benchmark against LFGB (German food and feed code) and FDA 21 CFR 176 alongside the retained EU requirements, because these give a common, supplier-independent yardstick for a grade that has no harmonised UK paper measure. Documentation scope is confirmed per SKU and destination market, as outlined on our [factory certificates](/factory-certificates/) page, which lists the third-party audits and test reports behind each supplied construction.

## PFAS, Evolving Chemical Rules and Retailer Standards

Two areas are moving faster than the statute book and deserve explicit attention in a UK sourcing programme. The first is PFAS: the Food Standards Agency has been reviewing per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances in the food system, and UK REACH — operated by the HSE since Brexit — runs its own evaluation pipelines that no longer automatically mirror EU decisions. Even without a binding UK restriction on fluorochemicals in paper to date, major retailers and foodservice brands increasingly write PFAS-free requirements into their packaging specifications, and buyers who require supplier declarations of intentional PFAS absence (backed by fluorine screening of the finished paper) are effectively future-proofing their range. Where a PFAS-free declaration is required, our [PFAS-free baking paper programme](/pfas-free-baking-paper/) documents the transition grade by grade.

The second is audit-based assurance. UK retail supply chains lean heavily on the BRCGS Packaging Materials standard, so a factory certificate to that standard — or FSSC 22000 / ISO 22000 with a packaging scope — usually carries more weight with a British buyer than any single test report. Buyers running seasonal or private-label programmes should also confirm how specification changes are controlled: a re-formulated coating or a new ink system voids the existing declaration until it is re-issued, and a supplier with disciplined change-control will flag the re-issue before the goods ship, not after.

## Labelling, Liability and Practical Programme Advice for UK Shipments

Labelling obligations are modest but easy to get wrong. The food-contact symbol or the phrase "for food contact" must appear where the consumer cannot otherwise tell the intended use — relevant for retail-ready parchment rolls and die-cut formats — and any environmental claims on retail packaging must be substantiated to the standard UK advertising rules expect, which means compostability and recyclability claims need the same documentary support as the compliance file itself. Liability in practice sits with the UK importer or brand owner, which is why sophisticated buyers treat the supplier's documentation pack as part of the product: a grade without a current, correctly scoped declaration is not a bargain, it is a liability transfer.

Operationally, the most efficient approach is to build a UK compliance checklist into onboarding: assimilated 1935/2004 conformance statement, DoC per construction, migration reports with the silicone and print systems identified, BfR XXXVI / AP(2002)1 reference or equivalent, PFAS statement, and BRCGS-grade factory certification. ## FAQ

### Does EU compliance documentation automatically satisfy UK requirements?
Almost, but not always. Great Britain works on assimilated Regulation (EC) No 1935/2004, so an EU DoC is largely transferable, but Northern Ireland applies EU law directly, UK REACH handles chemicals independently, and retailer standards such as BRCGS add audit expectations on top. Re-issue declarations under a UK-aware template and confirm scope per market.

### Is a declaration of compliance legally required for baking paper sold in Great Britain?
Yes in effect: Articles 16 and 17 of the assimilated regulation are retained, so specific materials in the supply chain must carry supporting declarations and traceability must be maintained. Retailers and auditors will request the DoC as a condition of listing regardless.

### Which test benchmarks should a UK buyer request for silicone-coated baking paper?
Overall and specific migration against the retained EU framework, plus LFGB and FDA 21 CFR 176 reports as widely recognised benchmarks for the grade. For paper-specific parameters, BfR Recommendation XXXVI and Council of Europe AP(2002)1 are the technical references most UK technical teams accept.

### How should PFAS-free claims be evidenced for UK retail programmes?
Combine a signed statement of intentional PFAS absence with fluorine screening of the finished paper, and keep both documents tied to the product specification version. This aligns with FSA scrutiny and satisfies retailer packaging policies that already prohibit intentionally added PFAS.
