## Introduction

Silicone coating weight — the mass of silicone applied per square metre of base paper, in g/m² — is the most misunderstood number on a baking paper spec sheet. Two suppliers can quote the same 40 gsm base paper at very different prices because one applies 0.7 g/m² and the other 1.5 g/m², changing release, multi-bake durability and cost per sheet. Buyers who treat coating weight as a price lever later receive containers that stick to caramel batters. Buyers comparing these specifications can review our [custom printed baking parchment paper](/products/baking-parchment-paper/) programme, where coating weight, base grammage and release values are documented per construction.

The working range for food-grade baking parchment runs roughly 0.7 to 1.5 g/m², set by the application, the base paper's grease resistance, and whether one or both faces are coated.

## What Silicone Coating Weight Actually Measures

Coating weight is the dry mass of cured silicone deposited per square metre of paper. The layer is thin and invisible — 0.7 g/m² on a 40 gsm paper adds under 2% to sheet mass — so it cannot be confirmed by weighing a sheet, and suppliers can under-apply invisibly.

Silicone's low-surface-energy layer stops batters and doughs bonding to the cellulose. Around 0.7 g/m², sheets give clean single-bake release for bread doughs and dry-goods tray liners; around 1.0 g/m², the film handles repeated contact and most cookie, cake and pastry work; at 1.2–1.5 g/m² it survives sticky, high-sugar systems and multiple bake cycles, where film damage becomes the failure mode.

Heavier coating does not raise the oven limit. Heat resistance is set by the base paper and silicone system, not layer thickness, so ask for the rated continuous-use temperature on the spec sheet.

## Matching Coating Weight to the Application

The cheapest error here is over-coating. A QSR chain lining pans for dinner rolls gains nothing above 0.8 g/m²; anything more is pure cost across millions of sheets. Under-coating costs more: a caramel wafer line at 0.7 g/m² shows sticking in the first bake cycle, and scrap and downtime exceed the savings many times over.

Frame the choice by failure risk: dry, fatty or immediately-lifted products tolerate the lower weights; sugar, moisture plus heat, or extended contact — honey cakes, fruit leather, praline — belong at the upper end. Between those poles, run a bake trial on your own product, scoring release, transfer and sheet condition.

## Single-Sided vs Double-Sided: Deciding Where the Coating Budget Goes

The next decision is distribution: one face or both. A 1.0 g/m² total can be 1.0 on one face, or 0.5 per face on a double-sided sheet. Single-sided concentrates release where the food sits; double-sided trades peak release on the food face for release on the handling face.

Map every surface contact: a muffin deliner, frame-fixed pan liner or tray divider touches food on one face only — single-sided coating at full weight gives the best release per dollar. Sheets flipped in production, used as interleaving, or handled on both faces need release on both sides, and total weight steps up to keep each face functional. ## How Suppliers Declare Coating Weight — and How Buyers Verify It

Coating weight claims are easy to print and hard to enforce unless the buyer pins down method, tolerance and per-batch evidence. A credible specification states the nominal value (1.0 g/m² ±0.1, for example), the method behind it — typically a gravimetric comparison between coated paper and uncoated substrate — and whether the value refers to each coated face or the total. That distinction alone explains many price comparisons that turn out to be apples versus oranges.

Verify in two stages. At sampling, ask for the coating weight record from the exact run, not a generic datasheet. At pre-production, bake a high-sugar reference batter on sample and current stock side by side, checking the contact face for gloss loss, transfer and tearing. In serial supply, require it on the batch certificate of analysis so drift becomes visible. The [factory and certificate library](/factory-certificates/) lists the third-party audits and test reports behind these claims. A supplier who resists declaring method and tolerance is telling you something about their process control.

## FAQ

### Does a heavier silicone coating always mean better release?

No. Release improves sharply up to about 1.0 g/m², then levels off; extra silicone beyond the film's integrity point adds cost without meaningful gain. Sticky applications are limited by film damage under heat and sugar, solved by adequate weight plus a suitable base paper.

### Is coating weight declared per face or for the whole sheet?

The specification must state which. A "1.0 g/m² double-coated" sheet could carry 0.5 g/m² per face or 1.0 per face — a two-fold cost difference. Require the per-face figure in writing.

### What coating weight should a cookie or cake program specify?

Most cookie, sponge and cake work sits at 0.9–1.1 g/m² on a grease-resistant base; move down for dry doughs and pan liners, up toward 1.3–1.5 g/m² for sugar-heavy or multi-bake use. Confirm with a bake trial.

### Does double-sided coating change food-contact compliance?

No. Both faces use the same food-contact silicone system; compliance documentation covers the finished construction, not each face. What changes is coating consumption and price.
