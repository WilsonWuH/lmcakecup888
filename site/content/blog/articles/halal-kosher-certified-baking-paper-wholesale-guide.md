## Introduction

Paper looks like the simplest item in a food packaging range, which is why buyers are often surprised when a halal or kosher customer asks for certification on a baking cup. The question is reasonable: a baking cup contacts food directly, is heated with it, and comes from a supply chain that includes coatings, release agents, processing aids and, in some constructions, recycled fibre. None of that is visible in the finished cup, but all of it is relevant to a religious dietary standard.

For a wholesale buyer the task is not to become an authority on dietary law. It is to know what a certificate does and does not cover, which bodies issue credible ones for paper products, which documents to collect before the first shipment, and how the requirement changes with the destination market. Buyers who treat certification as a one-page formality discover the gap at a customer audit, when fixing it costs most.

This guide covers halal and kosher certification for baking paper, baking cups and related food paper packaging: scope, issuing bodies, the supply-chain points where risk enters, the documents to request, and the differences between major export markets.

## 1. What Certification Actually Covers

A halal certificate addresses whether a product is permissible under Islamic law. For paper packaging the concern is not the fibre itself but what is added to it and what it has touched: animal-derived additives such as stearates or gelatine-based sizing, alcohol-based carriers in coatings or inks, and cross-contamination from shared equipment handling non-halal materials.

A kosher certificate addresses compliance with Jewish dietary law. The two concepts buyers need are pareve and equipment status. Pareve means the item contains no meat or dairy derived input and can be used with either; that is the status most baking paper buyers want. Equipment status matters because a product run on lines shared with non-kosher material may carry a different status even where the formulation is clean. Passover is a separate, stricter programme and must be requested explicitly if needed.

Both are scope documents: named products, at a named site, under a named programme, for a stated period. A certificate that does not name the SKU being purchased is not evidence for that SKU.

## 2. Issuing Bodies Buyers Will Meet

Halal certification is issued by national and private bodies, and recognition depends on the destination market:

- JAKIM (Department of Islamic Development Malaysia) — widely referenced across Southeast Asia and by many Gulf importers.
- BPJPH and MUI (Indonesia) — central to the Indonesian market, where halal assurance systems cover a wide product scope.
- GIMDES (Turkey) and other national schemes relevant to Turkish and Central Asian trade.
- IFANCA (Islamic Food and Nutrition Council of America) and the Halal Food Authority (UK) — common in Western retail supply chains.
- Gulf and regional standardisation bodies — relevant where shipments enter GCC markets.

Kosher certification is dominated by a small number of agencies whose symbols retail and foodservice buyers recognise:

- Orthodox Union (OU) — the most widely recognised symbol internationally, with a pareve designation and a separate Passover programme.
- OK Kosher Certification, Star-K, Kof-K and the Chicago Rabbinical Council (cRc) — widely accepted, particularly in North America.
- Local rabbinical authorities — sometimes required for specific communities or destination countries.

Ask which body issued the certificate and whether it is recognised by the buyer's customer or the importing authority. Recognition, not existence, is what makes a certificate commercially useful.

## 3. Where Risk Enters the Paper Supply Chain

Most failures are not formulation failures. They are documentation and scope failures. Five points deserve attention:

- Coatings and release agents. Silicone and other surface treatments can be carried in solvents or emulsion systems that need declaring. Ask for the coating to be named at finished-product level.
- Wet-end additives and sizing. Animal-derived stearates and gelatine-based sizes are the classic hidden inputs.
- Inks and varnishes on printed cups. Alcohol-based carriers and certain additives need review, especially where print contacts food indirectly through nesting.
- Recycled fibre. Its history is unknown, so recycled content and a halal or kosher claim must be reconciled explicitly rather than assumed compatible.
- Shared production. Where a mill or converter runs certified and non-certified work, ask how the programme controls changeover, scheduling and identification.

A supplier who can answer these five questions with records is generally one whose certificate will survive an audit.

## 4. Documents to Request from a Supplier

Build the file per SKU before the first order, not after the first complaint:

- A current halal and/or kosher certificate naming the product scope, production site, issuing body and validity dates.
- A product-specific declaration identifying base paper, coating, inks and any animal-derived or alcohol-based inputs, or confirming their absence.
- The pareve or equivalent status statement where kosher certification applies, plus an explicit Passover statement if the market needs it.
- A letter of authorisation if the buyer intends to reproduce the certification mark on retail packaging.
- Food-contact documentation for the destination market, kept alongside the religious certification because the two are separate obligations.
- Lot coding and traceability evidence linking shipped cartons to the certified production campaign.
- Change-notification terms covering coating chemistry, mill, additives and recycled fibre content.

Check three things on every certificate: the named scope covers the SKU, the site matches the factory shipping the goods, and the validity date covers the production period. Expired certificates on a supplier website are the most common problem buyers encounter.

## 5. Export Markets and Their Practical Requirements

Requirements vary by destination. Southeast Asian markets with Muslim-majority populations commonly expect a recognised national halal certificate and may require importer-side registration. Gulf markets usually accept recognised national schemes but increasingly ask for shipment-level documentation. Indonesia applies a halal assurance system across a wide product scope, so confirm requirements early.

For kosher, North American and European retail and foodservice customers usually accept the major agencies without additional import formalities, but Israeli and some community buyers may require a specific authority. Where one shipment serves several markets, certify to the strictest recognised requirement in the destination list rather than keeping parallel files.

Confirm whether certification is needed on the packaging at all. In some markets it applies to the food product and process, with packaging treated as a contact article; in others the customer's own standard requests it. Asking which they require avoids buying a programme that is not needed.

## Conclusion

Halal and kosher certification for baking paper and baking cups is a scope and documentation exercise. Establish what the certificate covers, confirm the issuing body is recognised in the destination market, check the five supply-chain points where hidden inputs and shared equipment create risk, and collect a per-SKU file with a current certificate, an input declaration, pareve or equivalent status, lot traceability and change-notification terms. Verify validity dates against the production period on every repeat order, and build the file before the first shipment rather than after a customer question.

### Does plain paper baking cup really need halal or kosher certification?

Often yes, because certification concerns the whole construction rather than the visible fibre. Coatings, release agents, wet-end additives, inks and shared equipment all fall within scope, and many customers request certification on food-contact articles even where the food carries its own certificate.

### What is pareve status and why does it matter for baking paper?

Pareve means the item contains no meat or dairy derived input and can therefore be used with either category. Most buyers of baking paper and baking cups want pareve status because it keeps the article usable across a full product range. Request the designation explicitly on the certificate.

### Is a certificate issued for one product valid for the whole range?

No. Certificates are scoped to named products produced at a named site under a stated programme. A certificate covering a plain parchment sheet does not cover a printed baking cup with a different coating. Check that the SKU being purchased appears in the scope.

### Can recycled fibre content conflict with halal or kosher certification?

It can, because the history of recovered fibre is unknown. Where a product carries recycled content, ask how the supplier reconciles it with the certification programme and whether the certifying body has accepted it. Do not assume the two claims are automatically compatible.

### Which issuing body should a buyer accept?

The one recognised by the destination customer or importing authority. For halal this is often a national body such as JAKIM or a scheme relevant to Indonesia or the Gulf; for kosher it is usually a major agency such as OU, OK, Star-K, Kof-K or cRc. Confirm recognition before accepting the document.
