## Introduction

Mineral oil hydrocarbons are the quiet compliance question in paper food packaging. They are not named in most purchasing specifications, most suppliers do not volunteer data, yet they sit at the center of the debate around recycled fiber in food contact materials — the same recycled fiber that sustainability programs increasingly ask buyers to favor. For wholesale purchasers of baking paper, cake cups, greaseproof liners and paper straws, mineral oil is no longer exotic: retailer quality standards and several national frameworks already reference it, and food categories with long shelf life and high fat content face the widest exposure. This guide explains what MOSH and MOAH are, why paper carries them, how major markets treat the risk, and which test methods and supplier questions produce defensible answers.

## 1. What MOSH and MOAH Are and Why Paper Carries Them

Mineral oil hydrocarbons enter food contact paper mainly from two sources: recycled fiber and processing aids. Recycled paperboard starts life as printed matter — newspapers, brochures, corrugated boxes — where mineral oil inks based on petroleum distillates were standard for decades. Those hydrocarbons survive repulping almost intact and end up distributed through the recycled sheet.

The substances divide into two families with very different toxicological profiles:

- **MOSH (mineral oil saturated hydrocarbons):** branched and cyclic alkanes that can accumulate in human tissue. EFSA's 2012 scientific opinion identified this accumulation as the main basis for concern.
- **MOAH (mineral oil aromatic hydrocarbons):** aromatic fractions that may include genotoxic and carcinogenic species. Because no safe threshold can be established with certainty, market expectation has moved toward "as low as reasonably achievable."

Additional sources matter at the margins: jute and sisal bags treated with batching oils, machine lubricants contacting the paper web, and some adhesives used in converting. Both the fiber source and the converting environment drive the result.

## 2. How Markets Regulate the Risk Today

No single harmonized EU limit covers mineral oil in all food contact paper. What exists is a patchwork buyers must navigate:

- **Framework level:** Regulation (EC) No 1935/2004 requires that materials do not transfer constituents in quantities that endanger health or unacceptably change food composition, and Regulation (EC) No 2023/2006 imposes GMP duties on converters. These duties apply to mineral oil transfers even without a numeric limit.
- **Germany:** the Consumer Goods Ordinance was amended to set binding limits on MOSH and MOAH in food contact articles made from recycled paper.
- **France:** national provisions set maximum mineral oil content in food contact materials placed on the French market.
- **Retail standards:** major European retailers operate private specifications with MOSH/MOAH limits for paper packaging of dry foods — chocolate, confectionery, bakery mixes and cereals are the classic categories.

For exporters, the defensible position is to test against the strictest relevant customer program rather than argue jurisdiction. Dry, long-shelf-life, high-fat foods transfer hydrocarbons most efficiently, so confectionery and bakery buyers see the tightest limits.

## 3. Testing Methods and What a Valid Report Looks Like

Method choice is where most buyer-side mistakes happen:

- **One-step GC-FID screening:** a fast gas chromatography screen that quantifies total mineral oil but cannot cleanly separate MOSH from MOAH. Useful for trend monitoring, insufficient for compliance decisions.
- **Two-step LC-GC:** the reference approach that isolates MOSH and MOAH fractions separately. Reports should state fractions individually in mg/kg with detection limits.
- **GCxGC confirmation:** two-dimensional chromatography used to characterize ambiguous MOAH results and distinguish them from natural hydrocarbons in the food itself.

A report is only as useful as its traceability. Require that every report identifies the exact article construction, the analytical method, the limit of quantification, the laboratory and the sample date. A generic "mineral oil: passed" statement attached to a different paper grade has no evidential value — the discipline described in the [migration test report review guide](https://www.lmcakecup.com/resources/food-paper-migration-test-report-review/) applies here.

## 4. Risk by Paper Type and Practical Mitigation

Mineral oil risk is not uniform across paper products, which lets buyers prioritize testing:

- **Virgin fiber greaseproof, glassine and silicone-coated baking paper:** the lowest-risk category. Virgin cellulose carries no printing history, so the recycled-newsprint pathway does not exist. Confirm virgin fiber at the mill and food-grade lubricants in converting.
- **Recycled paperboard articles — cake boards, cartons, tube cores:** the primary risk carrier, especially in direct or close contact with dry fatty foods.
- **Multi-layer constructions:** a functional barrier of virgin fiber or an approved barrier layer between a recycled body and the food can reduce transfer dramatically; barrier performance must be demonstrated by test, not asserted.

Mitigation options worth discussing with suppliers include moving the food contact side to virgin fiber, specifying barrier-coated recycled boards, and improving secondary packaging to increase separation. The [PFAS-free food paper transition guide](https://www.lmcakecup.com/resources/pfas-free-food-paper-packaging-transition-guide/) shows a comparable dynamic: consumer-facing claims and test evidence must move together.

## 5. RFQ Questions That Surface Mineral Oil Control

Add these lines to supplier questionnaires and score the answers:

- Is the food contact layer virgin fiber, recycled fiber, or a combination? State the recycled percentage.
- Which mineral oil test reports exist for the offered article: method, fractions, results, laboratory, date?
- For recycled constructions, what functional barrier is claimed and what data supports it?
- What food-grade lubricant and oil management practices apply at the converting stage?
- Will the supplier re-test on a defined frequency and notify changes in fiber sources?

Suppliers who answer fluently run mineral oil control as daily quality management. Suppliers who answer with brochure language are asking you to discover their limits during your customer's audit.

## FAQ

### Does all paper food packaging contain mineral oil hydrocarbons?

No. The dominant pathway is recycled fiber that once carried mineral-oil-based printing inks. Virgin fiber products — greaseproof, glassine and silicone-coated baking paper — start without that heritage load, though converting lubricants and adhesives must also be managed. Testing is the only confirmation.

### Are there legally binding MOSH/MOAH limits for food paper?

It depends on the market. The EU framework sets general safety and GMP duties without a harmonized limit, while Germany has binding limits for recycled-paper articles and France has national provisions. Retailer private specifications often impose the tightest requirements.

### Which food categories face the highest migration risk?

Dry foods with long shelf life and some fat content — chocolate, confectionery, biscuits, bakery mixes, cereals — transfer hydrocarbons most efficiently from paperboard. Long contact times and warm storage increase migration.

### What test method should I require from suppliers?

Require a two-step LC-GC method reporting MOSH and MOAH separately in mg/kg with stated detection limits, performed on the exact article construction offered. One-step GC-FID results are acceptable only as preliminary screening.

## Conclusion

Mineral oil hydrocarbons are a manageable specification issue, not a reason to abandon paper packaging or recycled content. Buyers who understand that risk concentrates in recycled fiber and dry fatty foods, require two-step LC-GC testing on the exact article, and push for functional barriers where recycled construction is needed can satisfy both regulatory scrutiny and retailer programs. Virgin fiber baking papers and cake cups sit at the low end of the risk spectrum, and documented converting hygiene keeps them there.
