## Introduction

Every food contact compliance conversation starts with the substances somebody chose to put in the product — the silicone oil, the wet-strength resin, the defoamer. The harder conversation is about the substances nobody chose: reaction byproducts from the resin itself, carryover from recycled fibre, degradation products formed when an additive meets a hot oven, trace solvents left from printing. Regulators group all of these under one term — non-intentionally added substances, or NIAS — and they are, by some distance, the most common reason a technically "compliant" paper fails a serious customer audit.

NIAS matter to wholesale buyers for a blunt commercial reason: a declaration of compliance lists intentional ingredients, but it does not prove their absence. Retailers and industrial bakery customers increasingly ask for NIAS evidence explicitly, and importers into the EU carry the legal duty to ensure food contact materials are safe under Regulation 1935/2004 — a duty that reaches substances nobody deliberately added. This guide explains what NIAS are, what European regulation expects, how screening and risk assessment work in the laboratory, and what a buyer should write into supplier requirements.

## 1. What NIAS Are and Where They Come From

The legal definition is elegant and unhelpful on its own: NIAS are chemical substances present in a food contact material but not added for any technical purpose. In paper manufacturing, they arrive through four main routes:

- **Reaction and degradation products.** Silicone curing leaves residual siloxane oligomers; wet-strength resins generate byproducts; some degradation products form only in service — a coating exposed to 200°C in a bakery oven can generate compounds that never existed in the delivered reel.
- **Impurities in intentional additives.** A technical-grade additive is rarely a single molecule. Side fractions and manufacturing residues in an otherwise approved substance count as NIAS even though the supplier added the substance legitimately.
- **Carryover from recycled fibre and process aids.** Recovered paper brings mineral oil hydrocarbons (MOSH/MOAH), inks, adhesives and residues from earlier lives.
- **Contaminants from converting.** Inks, lamination adhesives, machine oils and cleaning agents applied downstream of the mill each contribute substances the food contact assessment may never have considered.

NIAS are typically present at microgram levels, far below any certificate of analysis threshold. Managing them is a matter of process control and structured assessment, not of finding a supplier who claims to have none — no such supplier exists.

## 2. What Regulation and Guidance Expect

The EU framework places NIAS inside the general safety requirement of Regulation 1935/2004: materials must not transfer substances in quantities that endanger health or unacceptably change food. There is no harmonized EU positive list for paper and no NIAS-specific migration limit — which does not mean NIAS are unregulated. Regulation (EC) 2023/2006 requires good manufacturing practice, and GMP for NIAS means a documented risk assessment of the substances the process could plausibly generate or carry in. Germany's BfR Recommendation XXXVI on paper and board, together with the industry manifesto on process control, is the de facto benchmark most European customers apply.

Three principles recur across the guidance:

- **Risk assessment over blanket testing.** The expectation is a structured evaluation: identify substances of concern from the formulation and process, estimate exposure from migration and food contact conditions, and demonstrate that identified risks are controlled or excluded.
- **Thresholds of toxicological concern.** NIAS assessment commonly uses Cramer classification and TTC thresholds to decide which trace substances need full toxicological evaluation and which are negligible. Buyers do not need to run this mathematics themselves — but they need to know whether their supplier's dossier contains it.
- **Responsibility travels with the supply chain.** Each actor — pulp and paper producer, converter, importer — must communicate relevant composition and risk information. An importer of finished baking cups cannot outsource the legal safety duty by pointing at the converter.

Auditors increasingly ask not "do you have a test report" but "show me your NIAS risk assessment process" — regardless of origin.

## 3. How NIAS Screening and Assessment Actually Work

A credible NIAS evaluation is a combination of targeted and non-targeted analysis followed by toxicological interpretation:

1. **Formulation and process review.** The assessor lists every intentional substance, every plausible byproduct and every possible contaminant, then screens out those ruled out by exposure or by barrier between the substance and the food.
2. **Non-targeted screening.** Extraction of the material followed by GC-MS and LC-MS screening — with migration into food simulants under contact conditions matched to actual use (an ovenable baking paper demands far harsher conditions than a sandwich wrap).
3. **Identification and quantification.** Significant unknown peaks are identified against libraries and quantified semi-quantitatively; identified substances are compared against TTC thresholds and any relevant restrictions.
4. **Conclusion and control.** The dossier ends either in a safe-use conclusion or in corrective action: substituting an additive, changing a process parameter, or adding a functional barrier.

Buyers reviewing a supplier's NIAS documentation should look for the structure, not just the certificate: a screening chromatogram with a substance list, a rationale for exclusions, contact conditions that match the real application, and a named laboratory method. A single-page "NIAS: not detected" statement is worth very little — every material contains some NIAS, so a claim of absence signals that no real assessment was done.

## 4. What Buyers Should Demand from Suppliers

NIAS risk is managed through supplier requirements long before any test report arrives. A practical buyer requirement set looks like this:

- **A NIAS risk assessment dossier** for each food contact construction, referencing the formulation, process and the intended food contact conditions — updated when formulation or process changes, and linked to the change-notification procedure.
- **GMP certification or audit evidence** under a recognized scheme (BRCGS, FSSC 22000, ISO 22000) covering the converting stage, since converting is where many NIAS enter.
- **Fibre origin disclosure.** Virgin versus recycled content determines the dominant contaminant population; recycled grades should come with mineral oil and photoinitiator screening, and the grade should match the application — recycled fibre is a poor fit for direct contact with fatty or hot foods.
- **Low-migration converting practices**: food-grade or low-migration inks, curing discipline before stacking printed material, and separation of food contact surfaces from machine oil paths.
- **Change control**, because a NIAS assessment is only as current as the formulation it describes. A new defoamer or ink supplier restarts the assessment.

Across a supplier relationship, the pattern that protects buyers is consistency: the same construction, assessed once, with changes announced before they ship. NIAS problems rarely arrive as laboratory surprises; they arrive as undocumented changes.

## FAQ

### Are NIAS limits defined in EU law for paper packaging?

No. There is no harmonized EU positive list or numeric NIAS limit for paper. The general safety requirement of Regulation 1935/2004 applies, supported by GMP obligations under Regulation (EC) 2023/2006 and benchmarks such as BfR Recommendation XXXVI. In practice, customers enforce NIAS control through documented risk assessments.

### Does a food contact migration report already cover NIAS?

Usually not. A standard overall and targeted migration report measures listed substances only. NIAS are, by definition, unlisted — they require dedicated non-targeted screening (GC-MS/LC-MS) and a toxicological interpretation. Ask specifically whether the dossier includes NIAS screening; "migration test passed" does not answer the question.

### Is recycled paper riskier for NIAS than virgin fibre?

Yes, structurally. Recycled fibre carries the contaminant population of its previous lives — mineral oils, inks, adhesives — and demands more extensive screening. That does not make recycled paper non-compliant; it makes grade selection an application decision. For direct contact with hot or fatty foods, most buyers specify virgin or food-grade recovered grades with documented screening.

## Conclusion

NIAS sit at the edge of what a certificate can prove and at the center of what a serious customer will ask. They are unavoidable in any paper — the question is never whether they exist, but whether someone has assessed them, controlled them and documented the assessment. Buyers who understand the four sources, know what a credible dossier looks like and write the requirement into supplier agreements convert a vague compliance anxiety into a manageable, auditable process. That is precisely what the regulation expects and precisely what protects the importer who signs the declaration.
