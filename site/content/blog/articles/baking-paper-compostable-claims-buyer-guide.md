## Introduction

Few words on a baking paper spec sheet carry as much commercial weight — or as much ambiguity — as "compostable." The term has no single meaning unless it is tied to a standard, a test environment and a certificate covering the exact construction being sold, and a buyer who accepts an unqualified claim inherits the risk that it cannot be defended when a retailer, regulator or certification body asks for evidence.

This guide explains what compostability claims actually certify for baking paper, how industrial and home compostability differ, what happens physically to unbleached kraft and silicone-coated parchment in a composting environment, and which documents a wholesale buyer should require before the claim reaches their own packaging. The same evidence discipline that applies to compostability applies to the rest of the specification, which is why we publish test data behind every construction in our [silicone-coated parchment sheets and rolls](/products/baking-parchment-paper/) programme.

## Why Compostability Claims Matter in B2B Paper Sourcing

A compostability claim on baking paper is rarely consumed by the buyer alone: the paper usually ends up inside a branded product, so a weak claim at the paper level becomes an unsubstantiated claim at the retail level. When a supermarket chain audits its suppliers, it asks the brand for the certificate behind the environmental statement — and the brand asks the paper supplier.

The cost asymmetry is unfavourable: asking for documentation before the order costs a few emails, while withdrawing a printed claim after packaging runs means reprints and regulatory exposure. Consumer-protection authorities in the US, the EU and the UK have all acted against vague environmental packaging claims, and "compostable" printed without naming the standard and the test environment is exactly the wording their guidance targets. Buying paper whose claim is already documented shifts that risk upstream.

## EN 13432 and the Industrial vs Home Compostability Split

EN 13432 is the reference standard for industrial compostability in Europe, and a certificate to it means four things were tested: disintegration (the material breaks apart within 12 weeks in a controlled composting process), biodegradation (at least 90% of the organic carbon converts to CO₂ within about 180 days under thermophilic conditions around 58 °C), ecotoxicity (the resulting compost does not harm seed germination or plant growth), and low heavy-metal levels. Home compostability is a different, harder claim: OK compost HOME certification requires materials to degrade at ambient outdoor temperatures without the sustained heat of an industrial plant, which is why a product can pass industrial requirements and still not qualify for a home-compost label.

In North America the equivalent benchmark is ASTM D6400, with ASTM D6868 covering paper coatings intended for compostable packaging. The practical buying rule is simple: every compostability claim should name the standard, the certification body and the scope of the certificate. ## How Unbleached and Kraft Baking Paper Actually Degrades

Paper is cellulose fibre, and cellulose is inherently biodegradable — microorganisms in compost consume it readily, which is why unbleached kraft baking paper has a natural head start in any degradation discussion. What varies is everything added to the fibre: bleaching chemistry, wet-strength agents, sizing and release coatings all sit on or between the fibres, and a compostability certificate has to account for each of them.

Silicone-coated parchment deserves a specific note because buyers often assume the coating blocks compostability. The silicone release layer is applied at 0.7–1.5 g/m² — a small fraction of the total mass of a 35–45 gsm sheet — and silicone-coated constructions can and do achieve EN 13432 certification when the complete construction is tested. The key phrase is complete construction: the certificate is valid for the tested combination of base paper, coating and any additives, so a certificate held for one grade does not automatically cover a heavier coating or a different base. Certification is therefore a per-construction document, and the buyer's job is to match the certificate number to the grade on the order, not to the product family.

Certification also has limits: compostable does not mean the sheet disappears in a bakery's own waste stream, nor that it degrades in landfill, nor that printed versions behave like unprinted ones — ink systems are part of the construction and part of the test.

## Avoiding Greenwashing in B2B Marketing Claims

Greenwashing risk in paper packaging comes less from outright falsehood than from imprecise wording. "Biodegradable" without a timeframe and environment is the classic example — everything biodegrades eventually — while "100% natural" and "eco-friendly" attract the same regulatory attention. The FTC Green Guides and the EU's environmental-claims rules push in the same direction: qualify the claim, name the standard, and keep the evidence on file.

For a B2B buyer the safe pattern is to let the certificate speak. "Certified industrially compostable to EN 13432 by [named body], certificate valid for grade X" is defensible; "our compostable baking paper" is not. Push back too on claims that quote a standard's name without a certificate — standards are public documents, and citing one proves nothing about whether the product passed. ## Documentation Wholesale Buyers Should Demand

A defensible compostability file contains more than one PDF. Before committing volume, ask the supplier for: the EN 13432 or OK compost / ASTM certificate naming the certification body, scope and validity dates; the test report referencing the exact grade number and grammage you are ordering; the food-contact documentation (FDA or LFGB test reports, and a declaration of compliance under EU 1935/2004) confirming the same construction; and confirmation of whether printed versions are covered or whether ink systems change the certified scope. If the claim is home compostability, expect the corresponding home-compost certificate rather than an extrapolation from the industrial one.

Two habits make the file usable: tie every document to a grade code and re-confirm it whenever the specification changes, since a coating-weight adjustment can move a construction outside its tested scope; and diarise certificate renewal dates, because an archived PDF is not a live claim. Suppliers accustomed to export markets maintain this file as a matter of course — hesitation to provide it is itself useful information.

## FAQ

### Is all baking paper compostable?

No. Base paper is biodegradable cellulose, but coatings, wet-strength agents and ink systems determine whether a complete construction passes EN 13432 or a home-compost standard. Certification is per construction, so the claim must be verified for the specific grade.

### Can silicone-coated parchment be certified compostable?

Yes. The silicone release layer is a small fraction of the sheet mass (typically 0.7–1.5 g/m² on a 35–45 gsm base), and silicone-coated constructions can achieve EN 13432 certification when tested as a complete construction. Ask for the certificate naming the exact grade.

### What is the difference between EN 13432 and home compostability?

EN 13432 certifies disintegration and biodegradation under industrial composting conditions, including a thermophilic phase around 58 °C. Home compostability (such as OK compost HOME) requires degradation at ambient outdoor temperatures without that sustained heat, so it is a stricter claim that needs its own certificate.

### What documents should a wholesale buyer request?

The certificate naming the certification body, scope and validity dates; the test report referencing the exact grade and grammage; food-contact reports (FDA / LFGB) and an EU 1935/2004 declaration of compliance for the same construction; and written confirmation of whether printed versions are covered by the certified scope.

### Does "biodegradable" mean the same as "compostable" on packaging?

No. Biodegradable is an unqualified term unless a timeframe, environment and standard are stated, which is why regulators treat vague biodegradability claims as greenwashing. Prefer a claim tied to a named standard and a certificate covering the ordered grade.

## Conclusion

Compostability is a testable property, not a mood — and for baking paper the testing is precise about which construction, which environment and which standard is covered. EN 13432 answers the industrial-composting question, home-compost certification answers a separate and harder one, unbleached kraft degrades readily as cellulose, and silicone-coated parchment can certify when the complete construction passes. The buyer's leverage lies in demanding documents that match the ordered grade, keeping wording tied to certificates, and refreshing evidence as specifications evolve.
