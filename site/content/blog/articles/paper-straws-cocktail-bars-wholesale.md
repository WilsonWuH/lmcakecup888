## What Buyers Should Define Before Comparing Suppliers

Start the RFQ with a one-page requirement sheet. State the product name, intended food or beverage, direct or indirect contact, service or cooking condition, target quantity, destination, artwork status, pack format and inspection expectation. Add the dimensions that control fit: length, diameter, color, finish, glass fit, bundle count and presentation requirements. For a repeat order, attach the approved sample or drawing and identify which characteristics are critical to function, which are visual preferences and which are commercial options. This avoids a common sourcing error in which a supplier optimizes for an attractive sample while the buyer actually needs stable performance and repeatable carton control.

## Material, Food-Contact and Document Review

Material language should be specific. Ask the supplier to identify the paper, coating, ink, adhesive or support components that make up the finished item and to connect that description to the approved SKU. A general statement such as food grade, natural, recyclable or safe is not a substitute for a product-specific use statement. In the EU, Regulation (EC) No 1935/2004 sets general expectations for materials intended to contact food, including avoiding transfer that could endanger health or cause an unacceptable change in composition or organoleptic characteristics. Regulation (EC) No 2023/2006 addresses good manufacturing practice. In the United States, FDA food-contact status depends on the substance and its intended use. Buyers should therefore ask which legal basis, food type, time and temperature assumptions the supplier used, then retain the limitation with the approval record.

Where a current market claim is relevant, keep the claim narrower than the evidence. For example, a paper product can have a defined grease-resistant function without the buyer claiming a particular chemical, recycling route or certification that has not been documented for the finished item.

Internal review can be organized around four questions: Does the document identify the product? Does it state the intended food and conditions? Does it identify the legal or technical basis? Does it remain current for the supplier, factory and lot being purchased? If any answer is no, place the item in review rather than treating a logo or unsigned marketing paragraph as final approval.

## Sample and Application Testing

Do not approve the sample on a desk alone. Build a short application protocol using the buyer's actual cocktails, mocktails, garnish service, bar presentation and short but visible customer use. Record the supplier sample code, production date if available, food or beverage formula, equipment, temperature or storage condition, contact duration, operator, pack format and result. Inspect the item before use, during the critical step and after cooling, display, transport or service. Note release, odor, staining, deformation, tearing, count, fit, print set-off and customer-facing appearance as applicable. The objective is not to create an invented universal pass limit. It is to establish whether this specific construction performs against the buyer's own approved requirement and whether the result can be repeated with production-representative material.

A useful sequence is: receive and photograph the unopened sample; verify the label and count; measure the critical dimensions; run the agreed application with a control where practical; inspect the food-contact surface and the finished product; pack and move the result through the intended route; then compare the result with the approved drawing and commercial requirement. For paper straws, pay particular attention to a bar-service trial with ice, citrus, condensation, garnish and the actual glassware used by the buyer. If the supplier sends a revised sample, record what changed and repeat the relevant part of the trial. A buyer should not let a visually similar substitute enter the approval file without a new identity check.

## Packaging, Storage and Shipment

Packaging is part of the product decision because the paper or accessory must reach the buyer in the same condition as the approved sample. Confirm hygienic individual wraps or organized bundles that protect appearance behind the bar. Ask how inner packs are sealed, how counts are controlled, where lot codes appear, how cartons are palletized and what storage conditions the supplier expects. On receipt, photograph the pallet before opening, reconcile carton and inner-pack counts, inspect edge and surface condition, and keep one carton label with the receiving record. Where the item is sensitive to humidity, compression, odor or dust, agree the storage window and handling route before shipment rather than discovering the limit at the customer's production line.

## How to Evaluate the Supplier

Supplier comparison should use a weighted scorecard rather than a single quotation field. Score technical fit, application result, document quality, packaging, communication, corrective-action discipline and total landed risk. For this query, a supplier should be able to explain the approved construction, show how incoming and finished goods are checked, identify the records attached to a lot, and state how changes are communicated. A factory tour can be useful, but a tour is not a substitute for a controlled sample, a clear specification or evidence from the product being purchased. For hospitality groups, cocktail distributors, event caterers and premium beverage brands, the strongest supplier is the one that makes the buyer's own approval and repeat-order control easier.

## Inspection and Release Checklist

Before shipment, define an inspection plan in plain language. It can include identity, dimensions, appearance, print, count, carton condition, lot code and any application-specific checks. Do not insert a fixed AQL number or a guaranteed tolerance unless the buyer has agreed it with the supplier and the product drawing supports it. Instead, name the sample plan, the critical characteristics, the comparison standard and the disposition for a deviation. Keep photos of defects, measurements and the release decision. If an issue is found, quarantine the affected stock, identify the lot, preserve samples and request a root-cause and corrective-action response. This is more useful than accepting a general promise to improve.

Use this release checklist:

- Confirm the SKU, revision, material construction and approved sample.
- Check length, diameter, color, finish, glass fit, bundle count and presentation requirements against the current drawing or agreed reference.
- Verify the food, beverage, equipment, storage or service assumptions used in approval.
- Review the product-specific document file, revision date, limitations and lot link.
- Inspect inner packs, counts, carton labels, pallet condition and storage instructions.
- Record any deviation, hold or corrective action before the goods move into normal stock.

## Compare Quotes on Landed Risk, Not Unit Price Alone

When comparing quotations, separate the unit price from the cost of failure. Include tooling or artwork charges, sample and testing work, inner-pack and carton changes, freight cube, pallet utilization, receiving labor, likely scrap, line interruption, relabeling and the cost of repeating a sample. A more expensive construction can be the better commercial choice if it reduces unstable denesting, damaged presentation, customer complaints or rejected cartons, but that conclusion should come from the buyer's trial and records rather than an unsupported promise. Ask every supplier to quote the same approved specification and to list any assumption that is not included.

## Approval and Repeat-Order Workflow

A disciplined approval workflow is: (1) issue the requirement sheet; (2) collect comparable quotations and document lists; (3) receive samples with identity information; (4) inspect and test the samples in the real application; (5) resolve deviations; (6) sign the specification, artwork and pack-out; (7) approve the first production lot; and (8) monitor repeat shipments using retained samples and complaint records. That sequence is practical for global buyers because it keeps commercial, technical and compliance decisions connected. It also creates a clean handoff between sourcing, quality, production, warehouse and customer service.

## FAQ

### What should a buyer send with an RFQ for paper straws?
Send the intended use, food or beverage, destination, quantity, dimensions, artwork status, pack-out and the application conditions. For this category, include length, diameter, color, finish, glass fit, bundle count and presentation requirements. A supplier cannot responsibly choose a construction from a product name alone.

### Is a supplier declaration enough to approve the product?
It is one part of the file. Match the declaration to the finished sample, intended use, market and current revision, then complete the application and packaging checks that matter to the buyer. If the declaration has limitations, record them rather than treating it as a blanket certificate.

### How many samples should be tested?
Use enough production-representative pieces to observe normal variation and the buyer's actual process. The buyer should define the sample plan around the risk and the application, not accept an arbitrary number presented as universal. Keep representative retained samples from the approved lot.

### What is the most important supplier question?
Ask the supplier to explain how the approved construction is identified, inspected, packed and kept unchanged between the approved sample and repeat production. Then ask for records that support the answer.
