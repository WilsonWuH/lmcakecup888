## Introduction

Carbon reporting has moved down the supply chain. Food brands that once reported only their own operations now carry packaging in their Scope 3 inventory, and procurement teams are being asked a question that compliance files never required: what is the carbon footprint of this baking paper, and can you prove it? The uncomfortable answer from most of the market is "it depends" — because paper emissions vary enormously with energy mix, fiber sourcing and coating chemistry, and because life cycle assessment (LCA) data can be presented in ways that flatter or obscure. This guide explains which production stages actually dominate a baking paper footprint, how to read a product carbon footprint claim critically, what to request from suppliers, and how to build a sourcing strategy that improves real numbers instead of just reportable ones. Buyers comparing these specifications can review our [baking parchment paper manufacturing programme](/products/baking-parchment-paper/) programme, where coating weight, base grammage and release values are documented per construction.

## 1. Where Emissions Actually Come From in Baking Paper

A baking paper product accumulates emissions across a chain of stages, and the distribution is not intuitive. The dominant contributors are typically: For production volumes, the same construction logic applies to our [private-label parchment paper supplier](/products/baking-parchment-paper/) line, quoted per roll, sheet or die-cut format.

- **Pulp and paper production.** The mill stage — pulping, bleaching and drying the base paper — is usually the single largest hotspot. Energy intensity is high, and the energy source matters enormously: a mill running on bioenergy from process residues can have a radically different profile from a coal-fired one producing otherwise identical paper.
- **Silicone coating and curing.** Coating adds energy demand and the silicone polymer itself carries upstream emissions from its own production chain. Heavier coating weights mean more of both — one reason to match coating weight to the real application instead of defaulting to maximum release performance.
- **Chemicals and additives.** Sizing agents, wet-strength resins and release systems each carry embedded upstream emissions, generally small individually but non-trivial in sum.
- **Converting and transport.** Die cutting, sheeting and ocean freight are real but usually secondary — which is why "local production" alone is not a carbon strategy if the underlying mill is energy-intensive.

Buyers familiar with [FSC-certified sourcing](https://www.lmcakecup.com/resources/fsc-certified-baking-paper-sourcing-wholesale-guide/) should note that responsible fiber certification addresses forest impacts, not the mill energy profile — the two questions are related but separate. The [factory and certificate library](/factory-certificates/) lists the third-party audits and test reports behind these claims.

## 2. Reading an LCA or Product Carbon Footprint Claim

When a supplier presents carbon data, four questions separate a defensible number from marketing:

- **What methodology?** Credibility anchors are ISO 14040/14044 for full LCA and ISO 14067 for product carbon footprints, with sector guidance such as the paper industry's own frameworks adding boundary conventions. A claim with no stated standard is not a data point.
- **What boundary?** Cradle-to-gate (through leaving the factory) is the common basis for intermediate materials like baking paper. Cradle-to-grave adds distribution and end of life. Comparing one supplier's cradle-to-gate figure with another's cradle-to-grave is meaningless — insist on consistent boundaries before ranking suppliers.
- **What functional unit?** Numbers per tonne are standard, but packaging decisions happen per delivered function — per thousand sheets, per pallet, per bake cycle. A lighter grammage paper can win on carbon precisely because less material does the same job, which connects to the trade-offs in the [grammage buyer guide](https://www.lmcakecup.com/resources/baking-paper-grammage-buyer-guide/).
- **What is primary vs. secondary data?** Figures built from industry-average databases describe a hypothetical paper mill; figures built from the supplier's actual energy bills and chemical purchases describe the paper you buy. Ask which it is, and ask for the share of primary data.

A useful shorthand: the more specific and more boring the report, the more likely it is honest.

## 3. What to Ask Suppliers For

A practical data request to a baking paper supplier includes:

- **A product carbon footprint per functional unit** with stated methodology, boundary and data year — ideally for the specific grade, not a product family average.
- **Mill energy disclosure.** The energy mix of the producing mill (renewable share, fuel types) is the single most explanatory datum for paper emissions.
- **Recycled content and fiber origin data**, which feed both carbon and due-diligence requirements; for EU-bound goods these connect to the [EUDR due diligence process](https://www.lmcakecup.com/resources/eudr-paper-packaging-due-diligence/).
- **Evidence for any offsets or renewable energy certificates**, stated separately from the physical footprint. A footprint reduced only by purchased certificates is a different kind of claim than one reduced by process change — and under tightening green-claims regulation in the EU and elsewhere, the distinction is becoming legally significant.
- **A data roadmap.** Few suppliers have perfect data today. A supplier who can say "this is our current estimate, here is what we will measure next year" is often a better partner than one offering a polished but generic number.

## 4. Building a Lower-Carbon Sourcing Strategy

With data requests in motion, procurement can act on the levers that actually move baking paper emissions:

- **Rank mills, not just suppliers.** The same trading company may offer grades from several mills with very different energy profiles. Directing volume to the lower-carbon mill is often the largest single reduction available.
- **Optimize material use.** Right-sizing grammage, improving roll-width yield and reducing packaging-to-product ratio cut emissions and cost simultaneously; yield mechanics are covered in the [roll width and yield optimization guide](https://www.lmcakecup.com/resources/baking-paper-roll-width-yield-optimization-wholesale/).
- **Match coating weight to application.** A heavy-duty coating specified for greasy pastry overkills a dry-bread application, paying an emissions premium for unused performance.
- **Consolidate logistics sensibly.** Full-container shipments and efficient pallet patterns reduce transport emissions, but do not expect logistics to offset a mill-stage gap — the hotspot hierarchy puts converting and freight far below papermaking.
- **Document improvements for Scope 3 reporting.** Keep the data trail — old and new PCFs, assumption notes, supplier correspondence — so sustainability colleagues can defend the reported reduction rather than assert it.

Set expectations internally that carbon data matures over multiple procurement cycles. The goal of year one is comparable, boundary-consistent data from your main suppliers; the reductions come in years two and three.

## FAQ

### What is the typical carbon footprint of one tonne of baking paper?

Published ranges for coated paper grades vary widely — commonly from several hundred kilograms to well over a tonne of CO2e per tonne of product — because mill energy mix dominates. That spread is exactly why supplier-specific data matters more than any industry-average figure you might benchmark against.

### Is recycled-content baking paper always lower carbon?

Recycled fiber generally carries lower pulp-stage emissions than virgin fiber, but the full picture includes de-inking energy, the suitability of recycled grades for direct food contact, and downstream considerations. For food-contact baking paper, virgin fiber with strong mill energy data often outperforms — measure, do not assume.

### How does baking paper carbon data relate to Scope 3 reporting?

Packaging sits in Scope 3 Category 1 (purchased goods and services) for most food companies. Supplier PCFs built on primary data directly improve that inventory's quality, which is increasingly audited — another reason to prefer transparent methodology over headline numbers.

### What regulation affects carbon claims on packaging in the EU?

Beyond corporate sustainability reporting requirements, the EU is tightening green claims rules so that environmental assertions must be substantiated and verified. For buyers this means carbon claims made in marketing materials or tenders should rest on documented methodology — the same evidence discipline applied to [PFAS-free packaging claims](https://www.lmcakecup.com/resources/pfas-free-paper-packaging-evidence/).

## Conclusion

Baking paper's carbon footprint is decided mostly at the mill, shaped secondarily by coating weight and material efficiency, and only marginally by transport. Buyers who ask for methodology, boundary, functional unit and data quality — and who write those requirements into tenders — get comparable numbers they can rank, act on and defend. The winning sourcing strategy combines mill selection, grammage and coating right-sizing, and documented improvements that feed credible Scope 3 reporting year after year.
