## Introduction

Most food-contact paper packaging carries no expiry date — yet the compliance file behind it can go stale. A Declaration of Compliance (DoC) signed three years ago may reference a coating formulation the supplier no longer uses. A migration test report may have been valid for a grade that has since changed release agents. A customer audit can turn a tidy folder into a liability in one afternoon, simply by asking which documents still describe the material actually delivered. This guide explains how compliance files age, which events legally and commercially force retesting or reissuance, how to build an annual review cycle that catches problems before auditors do, and how to structure the file so any document can be found — and defended — in minutes.

## 1. Why Compliance Files Go Stale

A compliance file is a set of claims about a specific material, made at a specific time. Anything that changes either side can invalidate a claim:

- **The material changes.** Suppliers reformulate coatings, change fiber sources, swap release agents or move production to a different mill. Each change can alter migration behavior — and every migration test report describes the formulation as it was when tested.
- **The regulations move.** Food-contact frameworks evolve: EU plastics and food-contact legislation is periodically amended, US FDA expectations and state-level rules such as PFAS restrictions continue to develop, and import markets add their own requirements. A file compliant at signing can be outdated within a few regulatory cycles.
- **The use changes.** Your own side of the claim can shift. A paper specified for dry bakery use that you now use for a high-fat, hot-fill application no longer matches the contact conditions the original testing assumed.
- **The documents expire by policy.** Many test reports and declarations carry validity statements — commonly one to three years — or are issued per production lot. Knowing which documents carry which convention is the first step of file management.

The practical consequence: a file is never "done." It is a living record that must be kept synchronized with the material, the regulations and your use.

## 2. DoC Basics: What It Covers and What It Does Not

A Declaration of Compliance is the supplier's signed statement that the material meets applicable food-contact requirements — for paper packaging, typically anchored in frameworks such as [EU Regulation (EC) No 1935/2004](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX%3A02004R1935-20210327) for European markets and relevant FDA provisions such as [21 CFR 176.170](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-21/chapter-I/subchapter-B/part-176/subpart-B/section-176.170) for the United States. Three points about scope matter to buyers:

- **It is specific, not generic.** A valid DoC identifies the material or grade, the supplier entity, the applicable regulations and the intended conditions of use. A DoC that says only "our paper is food safe" documents almost nothing.

- **It is a chain, not a link.** Your DoC is only as good as the declarations behind it — the mill's declaration for the base paper, the coater's declaration for the silicone system, the ink supplier's statements for printed items. Reviewing the full chain is covered in the [supplier certificate verification guide](https://www.lmcakecup.com/resources/food-paper-supplier-certificate-verification/).
- **It carries conditions.** Many DoCs state limitations — maximum use temperature, fat-contact assumptions, single-use status. Using the material outside those conditions voids the coverage even if the document itself is current.

Buyers should also track who may issue or reissue a DoC: it must come from an authorized signatory at the supplying entity, and a reissued version must be traceable to a reason — a revision date with no stated cause is a red flag.

## 3. Retesting Triggers: When Migration and Compliance Tests Must Be Repeated

Rather than waiting for documents to lapse, responsible buyers retest or request updated testing when specific events occur:

- **Supplier change notification.** A formal notification of raw material, coating, ink or process changes is the primary trigger. Your change-control agreement with suppliers should oblige notification before shipments on the changed material arrive; the [paper packaging change control guide](https://www.lmcakecup.com/resources/paper-packaging-change-control-wholesale/) covers drafting those terms.
- **New or changed regulations.** When a market tightens requirements — new substance restrictions, lower migration limits, new documentation expectations — existing test reports should be reviewed against the new limits, with retesting where coverage is unclear.
- **Changed conditions of use.** Hotter ovens, fattier products, longer contact times or new markets all change the exposure profile the original tests assumed.

- **Periodic revalidation.** Even without triggers, most quality systems revalidate key migration and safety tests on a fixed cycle — commonly every one to three years depending on material stability and customer requirements. Set the cycle in writing and let the calendar, not memory, drive it.
- **Audit findings or complaints.** A customer audit finding, a complaint with a plausible migration dimension, or an industry alert about a substance in your material class warrants targeted retesting regardless of document age.

Deciding which tests to repeat is a scoping exercise with your supplier and laboratory: identify the changed parameter, then test for the substances and conditions it could plausibly affect, rather than repeating an entire test suite.

## 4. Building the Annual Review Cycle

An annual review keeps the file honest with modest effort. A workable cycle:

- **Quarterly: supplier document sweep.** Request current versions of DoCs, test reports and certificates from every active supplier; chase anything expiring within the next quarter.
- **Annually: full file audit.** For each packaging item, verify that the DoC matches the grade being delivered, that all underlying supplier declarations are current, that test reports are within their validity or revalidation cycle, and that conditions of use still match actual applications.
- **On event: delta review.** Regulatory updates and supplier change notifications trigger a focused review of only the affected documents and materials.

Keep a simple register — one row per packaging item — with the document, issue date, validity or revalidation due date, supplier, and status. The register turns compliance from archaeology into a dashboard.

## 5. Preparing for Customer and Authority Audits

Audits fail on organization more often than on substance. Before an audit:

- **Assemble by item, not by supplier.** Auditors ask about products, not vendors. For each packaging item, the file should present: DoC, supporting supplier declarations, relevant test reports, and the conditions-of-use statement — in that order.
- **Be able to trace a lot.** Pair the compliance file with your traceability records so any delivered lot links to its production date, supplier lot and applicable documents; the [traceability records guide](https://www.lmcakecup.com/resources/paper-baking-cup-traceability-records/) covers the linkage.
- **Close gaps before, not during.** If the annual review found an expired report or a missing declaration, either have the renewal in progress with dates, or a risk assessment explaining why the gap does not affect the delivered material. Auditors accept managed gaps; they reject surprises.
- **Rehearse the awkward answers.** Know how you will answer "when was this tested?" and "what changed since?" — the two questions that defeat most stale files.

## FAQ

### Does a Declaration of Compliance expire?

Some carry stated validity periods and many test reports do, but the more important rule is conditional validity: a DoC covers the material as described, under the conditions stated. Formulation changes, regulatory updates or changed use can make a technically "valid" DoC obsolete, which is why structured review cycles matter more than dates alone.

### How often should migration testing on food-contact paper be repeated?

Common practice is every one to three years without triggers, set by your quality system and customer requirements — plus immediate review when the supplier notifies a formulation or process change, when regulations change, or when your conditions of use change.

### Who is responsible for keeping the compliance file current?

The supplier is responsible for issuing accurate, current declarations and notifying changes; the buyer is responsible for holding a file that matches the material actually received and used. A written change-notification agreement converts the supplier's duty from goodwill into contract.

### What documents should be in the file for printed paper packaging?

The base DoC for the paper, ink system statements covering food-contact safety and migration, the printing supplier's declaration, and any tests specific to the printed surface — printed areas introduce ink and varnish migration questions that unprinted paper files do not cover.

## Conclusion

A food-contact compliance file is current only while it matches the material, the regulations and the way you use the product — three things that all change over time. Replace "when did we get this document?" with a cycle: quarterly document sweeps, an annual full-file audit, and event-driven reviews on every supplier change notification and regulatory update. Buyers who manage the file this way walk into audits with answers instead of explanations.
