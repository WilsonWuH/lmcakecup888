## Introduction

A small bakery chain ordering custom printed cake cups faces a balancing problem a single-location bakery never sees: every location bakes slightly different volumes, the print setup is a chain-level commitment, and the packing format has to work at the counter, in the stockroom and in the delivery van at the same time. Get one area wrong and the programme shows it quickly. A print scope one location cannot consume before the next reorder, a case pack that overwhelms a small stockroom, or location-level orders that fragment the chain's buying power. This guide works through the three decisions in order: consolidating order volume across locations, choosing print scope that scales with the chain, and specifying packing and distribution for a multi-site rollout. ## 1. Consolidating Order Volume Across Locations

The first decision is who orders. Location-level ordering feels safer to store managers, but it quietly dismantles the economics of a custom print: fragmented volumes re-trigger setup work, split the chain's buying power into smaller quotations, and often leave one location holding printed stock it cannot consume while another runs out. The workable alternative for a small chain is to consolidate demand at the chain level. Add up realistic monthly cup consumption per location from production or sales records, confirm which cup sizes each location actually bakes, and place one order sized to the chain's combined cycle rather than to each store's comfort. Where locations genuinely use different sizes, the consolidation target becomes the print programme rather than the SKU: one artwork, one colour standard, quoted across the sizes the chain needs, so the brand stays identical even when the cup formats differ. ## 2. Print Scope That Scales With the Chain

Print scope on a chain programme has to pass two tests: it must look consistent in every location, and it must be repeatable at every reorder without renegotiation. That argues for a restrained design. A logo and brand colour applied as a single or dual-colour print on the cup wall carries the brand at the counter, keeps plate and setup work manageable, and makes colour matching a controlled, documented exercise rather than a per-order negotiation. Chain programmes rely on the second impression matching the first, across sites and across months. Heavier multi-colour coverage is not forbidden, but it should earn its place: it adds fixed setup to every run, tightens registration tolerances on a curved cup wall, and enlarges the chain-level commitment each order size has to justify. Equally important is locking the specification once. Cup size, paper weight, print colours, artwork position and colour references recorded in a written spec, so that any location's reorder produces the same cup without a new approval round. A written spec also shortens the conversation when a new site opens: the cup arrives as a confirmed item rather than a fresh design discussion. ## 3. Packing, Denesting and Distribution for Multi-Site Rollout

Packing is where chain programmes quietly fail, because the pack that suits one location may not suit another and the supplier can only build what the specification says. Three questions settle it. Case pack quantity: sized so that a location's stockroom can store a full case without crowding and can consume it inside its normal usage cycle. Small locations with limited storage often prefer more, smaller cases even at a slightly higher packing cost per unit. Denesting behaviour: cups must separate cleanly by hand at the counter and, where locations use automated depositing, feed reliably from magazines. A cup that nests too tightly slows every service rush, while one that scatters loose in the case arrives dented and out of sequence. Transit and storage: printed cups travel to multiple addresses, so export-grade cartons, moisture protection inside the case and stackability on storage shelves all belong in the packing specification. A case that crushes under palletised freight delivers a location a shelf of unusable brand impressions. Distribution then becomes a routing decision, one consolidated delivery to a central point or split shipments addressed per location, and it should be fixed with the order so packing and labelling match the delivery plan. Chains adding new sites mid-programme should also decide now how a location change is handled: whether new sites join the standing specification automatically or trigger a fresh approval round, because the answer shapes how the artwork and colour standards are held. ## FAQ

### Should a small bakery chain order cake cups per location or consolidated?

Consolidate at the chain level wherever possible. Combined volumes carry the print setup once, produce stronger quotations and keep the brand consistent across sites. Locations with genuinely different cup sizes can stay in the same programme by consolidating the artwork and colour standard rather than the SKU.

### How much print scope is right for a multi-location cup programme?

A restrained one- or two-colour print of the logo in a brand colour usually scales best: it keeps setup work repeatable, makes colour matching a documented standard and holds the per-unit cost stable across reorders. Heavier coverage should be reserved for where customer-facing branding genuinely benefits, and judged against the larger fixed setup it adds to every run.

### What packing details should a chain cup order specify?

Case pack quantity matched to each location's storage and usage cycle, denesting performance for both hand service and automated feeders, export-grade cartons with moisture protection, and the delivery routing — central consolidation or split shipments — so packing and labelling match the distribution plan.

### What should a chain sample approval cover before rollout?

Print colour against the agreed standard, fit in the pans and ovens each location uses, release and clean demoulding on the actual product, denesting at the counter, and case pack and labelling against the distribution plan. Approval should be done on production-identical cups so rollout copies the sample exactly.

Chains that lock these into a written specification before the first order get predictable reorders and a brand that looks the same everywhere it bakes.
