## Introduction

Heavy metals are the quietest failure mode in paper food packaging. Grease resistance is visible on the line, and mineral oil migration is now widely discussed, but lead, cadmium, mercury and chromium can sit in a delivered lot with no sensory signal at all. They usually surface only when a retailer or importer asks for the documentation and finds it incomplete or out of date, by which point the packing run has often already started.

The topic also produces more confusion than almost any other compliance area, because the numbers quoted by suppliers frequently cannot be compared with each other. One paper cites total content in mg/kg, another reports migration into a food simulant, a third gives an extractive value against a national recommendation, and a fourth simply says "not detected" without a detection limit. All four may be honest and still useless for a purchasing decision.

This guide sets out which metals matter in paper food packaging, where they come from, how the main regulatory frameworks treat them, what a test result actually means, and what a buyer should require in every delivered lot.

## Which Metals Matter, and Why

The metals buyers are normally asked about are lead, cadmium, mercury and chromium, including hexavalent chromium. Lead and cadmium dominate retail requirements and state-level litigation in the United States; cadmium can also arrive through pigments and mineral filler contamination; chromium is relevant because some pigments and treatments introduce it and the oxidation state changes the risk profile. Arsenic, antimony and other elements are usually tested as part of a broad panel, driven more often by a customer or retailer specification than by a single regulation.

Two principles follow. Metals compliance is demonstrated against a defined panel and reporting basis, not a single headline number. And because food contact may be dry, fatty or moist, the relevant test depends on the intended use.

## Where the Metals Come From in Paper Packaging

Metals in paper packaging are rarely added deliberately. They arrive through materials and process routes:

- **Recycled fibre.** Recovered paper can carry legacy printing inks, coatings and fillers from previous use, and this is the single most important route for lead and cadmium appearing in paper and board.
- **Pigments, dyes and fillers.** Mineral fillers and some titanium dioxide grades, plus coloured pigments used in printing or in the sheet itself, can contain trace metals depending on origin and purity.
- **Printing inks and coatings.** Older pigment chemistries and some inorganic colourants are potential sources, which is why the ink system matters for direct-contact and near-contact applications.

The practical implication is that recycled content and colour intensity are the two specification choices that most affect metals risk.

## How the Main Regulatory Frameworks Treat Metals

There is no single global limit for heavy metals in paper food packaging. Compliance is assembled from general safety requirements, national measures for paper and board, and retailer or state-level specifications.

- **European Union.** Regulation (EC) No 1935/2004 requires that food contact materials do not transfer constituents in quantities that endanger health or unacceptably change the food, and Regulation (EC) No 2023/2006 sets good manufacturing practice. The EU has not adopted a harmonised specific measure for paper and board, so national provisions apply, including the German BfR Recommendation XXXVI and the Council of Europe paper and board guidance, which address certain metals alongside other substances.
- **United States.** Paper components for food contact are addressed through the FDA food-contact framework, including 21 CFR 176.170 for aqueous and fatty foods and 176.180 for dry foods. Separately, California Proposition 65 requires warnings for listed substances, including lead and cadmium, above defined exposure levels, and remains the practical driver behind many US packaging specifications for metals.
- **China.** GB 4806.8 covers food contact paper and paperboard, supported by GB 9685 for additives and the GB 31604 test-method series. These are commonly required for the domestic market and are frequently applied by importers as a condition of acceptance.
- **Customer and retailer standards.** In practice, the tightest requirement usually comes from a retailer specification or a brand's restricted substances list, which defines the panel, the limits and the acceptable reporting basis.

Because frameworks differ, a statement such as "heavy metals compliant" is not a specification. The buyer must name the framework, the panel and the limits that apply to that product and market.

## What a Test Result Actually Means

Results are only comparable when three things match: what was measured, how it was extracted, and what the result is reported against.

- **Total content versus migration or extraction.** Total content, in mg/kg of paper, describes what is in the material. Extractive or migration testing describes what could move into food under defined conditions. For dry baked goods contact, water and acid extracts are common; for fatty contact, a suitable fat simulant is used.
- **The reporting basis.** Milligrams per kilogram of paper, per square decimetre of surface, and per kilogram of food or simulant cannot be compared directly. A low total content figure does not automatically mean low migration for every food type.
- **Detection and quantification limits.** "Not detected" is meaningless without the method's limit of detection. A panel reported well below the applicable requirement is far stronger evidence than an unattributed negative result.
- **The laboratory.** Results from a laboratory operating to a recognised quality system, such as ISO/IEC 17025 accreditation for the relevant method, carry weight in an audit or retailer review. Inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry is typical for metals panels.
- **The sample.** Confirm whether the report covers the production lot supplied or a development sample. A report naming a different lot, an earlier specification or a superseded ink system is not evidence for the delivery in front of you.

A supplier who understands these distinctions will volunteer the method, conditions and limits alongside the numbers; one who cannot explain the basis of the numbers is a documentation risk regardless of the values.

## Supplier Documentation and Lot Verification

A workable compliance routine looks like this:

1. **Define the requirement in the specification**, naming the framework, panel, limits and acceptable reporting basis for each product and destination market.
2. **Request a declaration of compliance** naming the material, coatings and ink system, with supporting test reports attached rather than merely referenced.
3. **Qualify the material with a full panel** at the start of the relationship, from a lot you can trace.
4. **Set a lot or campaign re-test frequency** based on risk. Recycled fibre and heavily pigmented sheets warrant more frequent checks than a stable virgin-fibre grade.
5. **Keep a retained sample per lot**, so an issue can be traced without freezing the entire range.
6. **Verify changes.** Any change of fibre source, pigment, ink system or coating is a trigger for re-testing, because metals risk travels with materials rather than processes.

If a supplier cannot provide the declaration, the test reports or a traceable lot reference, treat that as a substantive risk rather than an administrative gap.

## Reducing Metals Risk by Design

Three design decisions do most of the work. A controlled fibre strategy, where the application allows, reduces the legacy-ink route for lead and cadmium. Keeping bright or heavily loaded colourants away from the food-contact face, or choosing pigment systems with supporting documentation, limits the most common inorganic source. Specifying the ink and coating explicitly, including which face contacts food, keeps the compliance argument simple and auditable.

None of these choices is free, and none is always necessary, but they should be made deliberately rather than discovered during an audit.

## What to Put in the Specification

- Named framework, metals panel and limits for each destination market.
- Reporting basis required: total content, extractive or migration, with method and conditions.
- Detection and quantification limits acceptable for the requirement.
- Requirement that reports name the production lot, the specification and the ink and coating system.
- Re-test triggers: change of fibre source, pigment, ink, coating or supplier site.
- Retained sample requirement and retention period, plus a declaration of compliance naming every food-contact material.

## FAQ

### Do all paper food packaging products need heavy metals testing?

Not at the same depth. A stable virgin-fibre, unprinted sheet for dry contact carries a lower metals risk than a brightly printed sheet with high recycled content. Most buyers still qualify a new material with a full panel, then set a reduced lot frequency for low-risk products based on the destination market's requirements.

### Is recycled paper unsafe for food contact?

Recycled fibre is not automatically unsuitable, but it is the main route by which legacy inks and fillers introduce lead and cadmium. Recycled grades can be used in food contact where the incoming material is controlled and the finished product is tested against the applicable requirements for the intended food type.

### What is the difference between total content and migration testing?

Total content measures how much of a metal is present in the paper, usually in mg/kg of material. Migration or extractive testing measures how much can transfer into food or a simulant under defined time and temperature conditions. Only the second directly addresses exposure, and the two cannot be compared without conversion and a defined basis.

### Why does a supplier say "not detected" and my retailer still reject the report?

Because "not detected" without a stated detection limit and method tells the reviewer nothing about whether the test was sensitive enough for the applicable requirement. Reports should state the method, conditions, detection and quantification limits, and the sample lot, so the result can be assessed against a specific limit.

## Conclusion

Heavy metals in paper food packaging are a materials-and-documentation problem, not a process one. Identify the panel and limits for each destination market, understand whether a result is total content or migration, require reports with stated methods, limits and lot references, and re-test whenever fibre, pigment, ink or coating changes.
