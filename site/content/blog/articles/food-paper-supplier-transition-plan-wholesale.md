## Introduction

Switching a food-contact paper supplier is one of those projects that looks simple in a boardroom and turns messy on the production floor. The incumbent's paper runs fine on your machines; the new supplier's certificate package looks equally good; and yet six weeks into the change, a muffin liner starts sticking, a case count is short, a document is missing from an audit file, and the whole initiative acquires a reputation for risk. The difference between a smooth migration and a painful one is almost never product quality alone — it is whether the buyer ran a structured transition plan.

This guide lays out a practical sequence for moving baking paper, cake cups, or greaseproof sheets from an incumbent supplier to a new one with minimal disruption. It is written for food manufacturers, industrial and retail bakeries, and foodservice groups in Europe and North America, and it assumes the commercial decision to switch has already been made or is being seriously evaluated.

## Why Transitions Fail

Most supplier switches that go wrong share the same failure modes. The first is running a single-step changeover: one day the old paper, the next day the new, with no parallel period. Any undetected difference in release performance, dimensional tolerance, or machine handling then surfaces as line downtime or consumer complaints at full volume. The second is incomplete documentation — the new supplier's food-contact compliance file arrives after production has started, and a customer audit or retailer requirement exposes the gap. The third is specification by sample: the buyer approved one golden sample, but the production shipment varies within a tolerance the trial never tested. A transition plan exists precisely to force these issues into the open while the incumbent can still cover demand.

## Phase 1: Define the Specification and the Exit Criteria

Before contacting the new supplier, write down what "equal or better" means in numbers. Pull the incumbent's current specification: grammage and tolerance, silicone coating weight both sides, tensile and burst figures where relevant, width and diameter or sheet dimensions, packaging configuration, pallet pattern, and shelf-life or storage claims. Add the compliance file: food-contact declarations for the destination markets, migration test reports, and any customer-specific requirements flowing down from your retail customers.

Then set exit criteria for the project itself — target dates for first trial, first production shipment, full volume migration, and the conditions that would pause or reverse the switch. Publishing these internally keeps the transition from drifting for quarters.

## Phase 2: Qualify the New Supplier

### Document Review Before Samples

Ask the candidate supplier for the full compliance package before anyone ships cartons: food-contact declarations, third-party migration test reports against the relevant regulations, factory certifications such as ISO 9001 or BRCGS packaging materials, and recent audit reports. Reviewing paper first costs nothing; discovering a missing document after the first pallet arrives costs a storage bay and a schedule. Verify that the reported product matches your spec — a generic declaration covering "greaseproof paper" is not the same as one naming your grammage and coating.

### Sample Evaluation and Line Trials

Run samples through a defined protocol rather than an informal look-and-bake. Typical checkpoints: dimensional check against spec, baking release over repeated bakes at your line's temperatures, color and odor of the baked product, machine runnability at production speed, and case/pallet condition on arrival. Where your lines differ — deck oven versus tunnel, manual versus automated denesting — trial on the most demanding line, not the easiest one. Keep retain samples from every trial lot; they anchor any later dispute.

Run the trial under normal production conditions with enough volume to expose variability — a single roll tells you less than a shift's worth of running time. Record machine settings that needed adjustment, because those become the transition training notes for your operators.

## Phase 3: The Parallel Run

The heart of a safe transition is a parallel period in which both suppliers ship and both products run on your lines. Structure it deliberately. Start with a small split — for example 80% incumbent, 20% new — concentrated on one line or one product family with the fastest feedback loop. Hold the split for a defined period or volume, review quality data and complaints, then step the new supplier up: 50/50, then 80/20, then full migration. The incumbent's volumes shrink on the same schedule, which also protects you commercially during the negotiation of any final orders.

During the parallel run, tighten the feedback loop: a daily line report during the first weeks, a named quality contact at both suppliers, and an agreed method for logging and classifying any issue (dimensional, functional, documentation, delivery). Issues found at 20% share are data; the same issues found at 100% are incidents.

## Phase 4: Documentation, Change Control, and Customer Notifications

A supplier change on food-contact material is a change under most food safety management systems, so it must flow through your change control process: update the raw material specification, supplier approval records, risk assessment, and any HACCP documentation that references the packaging. Notify customers where contracts require it — many retail and foodservice agreements mandate advance notice of packaging material changes, and some require the new compliance file before the switch takes effect in their stores.

Build the new supplier's file to the same standard as the incumbent's: declarations, migration reports, certificates of analysis per lot, and traceability records. Decide the retention sample policy for the new supplier's lots before production starts, not after the first claim.

## Phase 5: Ramp, Stabilize, and Close Out

After full migration, keep monitoring for a defined stabilization window — typically one to three months — tracking line efficiency, complaint rates, and delivery performance against the incumbent's baseline. Hold a final shipment of the incumbent's material as a contingency buffer until the new supplier has delivered reliably through at least one full replenishment cycle. Then close the project with a short review: what the trials missed, what the new supplier's communication is like in practice, and whether the promised commercial terms actually landed on the invoices. Document the lessons; the next transition will be easier for it.

## Common Pitfalls and How to Avoid Them

- Approving on a golden sample instead of production-representative lots; always trial material from a production run, not a lab batch.
- Ignoring secondary differences: carton markings, pallet heights, and roll core sizes that jam your dispensers cause as much disruption as paper defects.
- Letting the incumbent know nothing until the last order; unless there is a specific reason, a professional wind-down preserves a fallback source.
- Skipping a trial on seasonal or high-fat products, where release performance differences show up first.

## FAQ

### How long should a food paper supplier transition take?

For a single product family, six to twelve weeks from first trial to full migration is realistic: two to three weeks for documents and sample evaluation, two to four for line trials, and a parallel run of four or more weeks depending on volume and risk tolerance. Multi-site or multi-SKU programs run longer and should migrate product by product rather than all at once.

### Should I tell my incumbent supplier I am switching?

Commercially you must honor contract terms, but operationally a managed wind-down benefits both sides: the incumbent can plan final orders and support the parallel period. Most buyers inform the incumbent once the new supplier has passed trials, keeping the relationship professional enough that it remains a viable fallback.

### What documents must the new supplier provide before first shipment?

At minimum: food-contact declarations for your destination markets, migration test reports for the specific product, quality certifications (ISO 9001 or a GFSI-recognized packaging standard such as BRCGS), and per-lot certificates of analysis. Retail customers may add their own flow-down requirements — collect those before the parallel run starts.

### What is the safest volume split during a parallel run?

There is no universal split, but a common pattern is 80/20 incumbent/new, then 50/50, then 80/20 in the new supplier's favor, holding each step for a defined period with quality review gates between steps. The exact numbers matter less than the principle: increase exposure only after the previous step's data is clean.

## Conclusion

A supplier switch on food-contact paper is a project with a known shape: specify, qualify, parallel, document, ramp. The buyers who struggle are the ones who compress the sequence into a single handover and discover differences at full production volume; the buyers who succeed treat the incumbent as a safety net during a measured ramp and force every difference — dimensional, functional, documentary — into the open while volumes are still low. Done well, the transition does not just avoid disruption; it leaves you with a second qualified source, a sharper specification, and leverage that lasts well beyond the first invoice from the new supplier.
