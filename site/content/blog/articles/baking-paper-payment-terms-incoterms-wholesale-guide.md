## Introduction

Most wholesale baking paper buyers spend their negotiating energy on price, grammage, and sheet size — then sign the trade terms almost as an afterthought. That is a mistake. Payment terms and Incoterms determine when risk transfers, who pays for freight and insurance, what happens when a carton is damaged in transit, and how much working capital an order consumes before a single sheet arrives. On a container-load order, a poorly chosen term can cost more than the discount you negotiated on unit price.

This guide walks through the two halves of every international baking paper purchase: how you pay, and who carries the goods, costs, and risk at each stage of the journey. It is written for importers, distributors, bakery groups, and private-label buyers sourcing baking paper, cake cups, and food packaging papers from overseas manufacturers. Nothing here is legal advice; the goal is to make you conversant enough to ask the right questions before you sign.

## Payment Terms: The Four Structures That Matter

### Telegraphic Transfer (TT) — Deposit and Balance

TT against deposit and balance dominates the packaging trade: typically 30% to start production and 70% against a copy of the bill of lading. The split protects both sides — the supplier covers raw material purchases (custom-printed orders cannot be resold), while the buyer pays the balance only when shipped goods are evidenced by documents.

Buyer protections worth negotiating: pay the deposit only to the bank account matching the contract entity; require shipping documents before the final transfer; and for first orders, make a third-party inspection report a condition of paying the balance. Also agree up front what happens to the deposit if production is cancelled — easier to write that clause before paying than to negotiate it after a dispute.

### Letter of Credit (LC)

An LC shifts payment risk to the banking system: your bank commits to pay when a defined set of documents is presented. LCs suit large orders, new supplier relationships where neither side will carry deposit risk, and markets where currency regulations complicate direct transfers. The trade-offs: bank fees, strict document requirements (even a name discrepancy can delay payment), and slower settlement. Have your forwarder or a document specialist review the required document list before the LC is opened — correcting terms after issuance is slow and costly.

### Open Account and Other Mechanisms

Under open account terms the supplier ships and is paid later — net 30 to 60 days, for example. This is generally reserved for long-standing customers or large buyers with credit insurance; if offered early, verify the cost is not simply priced in. A common compromise is partial open account once a relationship has history. Marketplace escrow and trade assurance products sit between TT and LC: useful for trial orders below the size where an LC justifies its overhead, usually unnecessary for repeat volume with a proven supplier.

## Incoterms: Who Carries What, and Until When

Incoterms are standardized three-letter rules published by the International Chamber of Commerce that allocate transport costs, risk transfer, and customs responsibilities between buyer and seller. The terms baking paper buyers encounter most:

**EXW (Ex Works):** the buyer collects from the supplier's factory and handles export clearance, local pickup, and all onward logistics — usually uneconomical for a foreign buyer without a local agent. Ask for a local-cost quotation before agreeing to EXW.

**FOB (Free On Board):** the supplier delivers goods on board the vessel at the named port of loading, cleared for export; the buyer pays ocean freight and insurance from that point. FOB is the workhorse term for sea freight imports and suits buyers who have their own forwarder, because it separates the supplier's visible local costs from the international leg under the buyer's control.

**CFR and CIF (Cost and Freight / Cost, Insurance and Freight):** the supplier pays freight to the destination port — plus minimum-cover insurance under CIF. Risk, however, still transfers on board at the origin port: under CIF, a container lost mid-ocean is the buyer's loss, compensated through the supplier-arranged policy. CIF suits buyers without freight purchasing power, but compare all-in costs; freight-inclusive pricing can hide margin.

**DAP and DDP (Delivered at Place / Delivered Duty Paid):** the supplier carries goods and risk to a named destination, with DDP adding import clearance and duties. Maximum convenience, maximum supplier obligation — and therefore the most expensive term, requiring capabilities many manufacturers cannot provide directly. DDP can be practical for LCL trial shipments; for recurring container volume, FOB with your own customs broker usually lands cheaper with better visibility.

The decision rule: choose the term that keeps the leg you are best equipped to manage under your control. If you have a trusted forwarder at origin, FOB. If not, CIF or DAP for early orders, migrating to FOB as volume justifies it.

## How Payment Terms and Incoterms Interact

The two structures are not independent. Deposit-heavy payment concentrates buyer risk before shipment, so a documentation condition on the balance (bill of lading copy, inspection report) matters more under EXW or FOB. Under CIF or DAP, verify the insurance arranged covers your goods' value — ICC(A) cover is the standard buyers should expect, not a bare minimum policy. Whatever the term, align every payment trigger with a document that proves performance: deposit against order confirmation, balance against shipping documents or passed inspection — never against a verbal ship notice.

## Practical Checklist Before You Sign

- Confirm the contract entity and bank account match; be wary of last-minute account changes by email.
- State the Incoterm with the named place and edition year — "FOB Shanghai, Incoterms 2020" is a term; "FOB" alone is a dispute.
- Define the balance payment trigger precisely: which documents, against what evidence, within how many days.
- For LCs, review the document list before issuance and budget for fees and correction time.
- Agree insurance terms in writing where the supplier arranges cover, and clarify who pays for pre-shipment inspection.

## FAQ

### Which Incoterm is best for a first container of baking paper from an overseas supplier?

FOB is the most common recommendation for buyers with an experienced freight forwarder, since risk transfers on board at the origin port and the buyer controls ocean freight. Without a forwarder, CIF or DAP for early orders trades cost visibility for convenience; move to FOB as volume grows.

### Is a 30% deposit standard when buying baking paper wholesale?

Deposit-plus-balance TT is the prevailing structure, and 30% is a typical deposit for made-to-order goods, but it is negotiable. What matters more than the percentage is that the deposit goes to the verified contract entity and the balance is conditioned on shipping documents or a passed inspection.

### When should I use a letter of credit instead of TT?

LCs suit large orders, first transactions where neither party will carry deposit risk, and markets with currency transfer restrictions. They cost bank fees and demand strict document accuracy. For routine repeat orders with a proven supplier, TT against documents is usually faster and cheaper.

### Does CIF mean the supplier is responsible if goods are damaged at sea?

No. Under CIF, risk transfers to the buyer when goods are loaded on board at the origin port, even though the supplier paid freight and insurance. Damage in transit is handled through the insurance policy the supplier arranged — which is why buyers should confirm the coverage level in the contract.

### What does DDP include, and why is it more expensive?

DDP places every obligation on the supplier: freight, insurance, import clearance, duties, and taxes, delivered to your door. Suppliers price that obligation and risk into the quote, and many manufacturers cannot handle foreign import formalities directly. It is best suited to trial shipments and markets where the buyer lacks customs capability.

## Conclusion

Trade terms are not fine print; they are the plumbing of your supply chain, and they are fully negotiable. On the payment side, match the structure to the trust level: deposits and document conditions for new relationships, TT against documents for steady volume, LCs for large or complicated transactions. On the delivery side, pick the Incoterm that puts each leg of the journey in the hands of whoever manages it best — usually FOB once you have a forwarder, CIF or DAP before that. Write the named place and edition year into every contract, condition every payment on documents that prove performance, and revisit the terms annually as your volume grows. Buyers who master this vocabulary consistently land better total costs than those who negotiate only the unit price.
