## Introduction

Landed cost is where many US packaging import programs are won or lost. A cake cup or baking paper program can carry a factory price that looks unbeatable, then arrive with a customs bill that erases the margin — because the product was classified under the wrong HTS code, missed a preferential qualification, or was swept into a trade remedy investigation nobody tracked. Unlike many sourcing topics, tariffs are not something the supplier can fully manage for you: the importer of record owns the classification and pays the duty. Cup programmes usually ship alongside pan lining; our [custom printed baking parchment paper](/products/baking-parchment-paper/) specs cover the sheets and rolls that pair with them.

This guide explains how wholesale buyers of baking cups, muffin liners, baking paper and related food packaging paper should approach US customs: finding the right HTSUS heading, understanding what tariffs and trade remedies currently apply to paper products from major origin countries, and building classification and documentation discipline into the procurement process. ## 1. Classifying Baking Cups and Baking Paper Under the HTSUS

US customs classification follows the Harmonized Tariff Schedule of the United States (HTSUS), and paper food packaging items typically fall under Chapter 48 (paper and paperboard) rather than the bakeware chapters, because the substrate is paper: Where a bakery lines trays and moulds product in the same run, our [silicone-coated parchment sheets and rolls](/products/baking-parchment-paper/) are quoted with the cup programme for a single documentation package.

- **Muffin and cake cups, baking liners** are generally classified under heading 4823, which covers paper articles not elsewhere specified — the subheading depends on the product details and coating. This is also the heading where much parchment and greaseproof paper lands.
- **Baking paper rolls and sheets** typically classify under heading 4811 (paper coated, impregnated or covered with substances such as silicone), with the subheading reflecting whether the paper is bleached and its form.
- **Napkins, towels and wrapping paper** for foodservice use fall under their own 4818/4819 headings.
- **Composite items** — for example, a paper cup with a plastic coating — require careful reading of the legal notes, because treatment can depend on whether the coating is considered a covering or a change of essence.

Classification is determined by product reality, not by the supplier's invoice description. "Baking cup" on a commercial invoice is not a classification; the material, coating, dimensions and function are. Importers who classify from supplier descriptions routinely overpay duty — or underpay, which accumulates penalties and back-duty exposure. The [factory and certificate library](/factory-certificates/) lists the third-party audits and test reports behind these claims.

## 2. The Duty Stack: MFN, Section 301 and Beyond

The duty an importer actually pays is a stack of separate layers:

1. **MFN (Column 1) general duty** — the standard HTSUS rate for the classification, often modest or free for paper goods, but not always zero.
2. **Section 301 tariffs** — additional duties on specified Chinese-origin goods, adjusted by lists over time. Paper products from China have appeared on these lists, and rates have changed repeatedly, so the applicable list and rate must be checked against the current Federal Register notice at the time of entry — not against a quote from two years ago.
3. **Reciprocal and emergency tariff actions** — recent years have added additional country- and product-specific tariff measures that change frequently; importers need a routine for monitoring Federal Register and CBP CSMS messages.
4. **Anti-dumping (AD) and countervailing (CVD) duties** — separate from the above, imposed on specific products from specific countries when the US International Trade Commission and the Department of Commerce find injurious dumping or subsidization. Certain paper product categories have a long history of AD/CVD orders (thermal paper, uncoated paper and others), and any new investigation covering a packaging paper category can change landed cost by double-digit percentages almost overnight.

The practical consequence: a quote comparison between suppliers in different countries is meaningless without applying the full duty stack per origin. A higher factory price from one country can be cheaper landed than a lower price from another once tariffs are added.

## 3. Trade Remedies in Practice: AD/CVD Risk Management

AD/CVD is the layer importers most often overlook, and the one that moves fastest:

- **Scope is product- and country-specific.** An AD order on a paper category from one country does not touch the same product from another. Scope rulings determine whether your product is covered — the written scope language, not the case's popular name, decides.
- **Cash deposit rates follow the entry, not the final decision.** Once an investigation begins, entries on or after a specified date may be subject to retroactive duties if the final determination is affirmative. Importers with goods on the water when a case initiates carry real exposure.
- **The screening habit.** Serious importers screen their portfolio against pending petitions published by the ITC and Commerce and, where exposure is plausible, budget a contingent duty reserve and track investigation timelines. Importer of record status means the bill lands on you, even if the supplier promised otherwise.

Buyers sourcing through trading companies should note that AD/CVD follows the country of manufacture and the product, not the exporter's identity — changing the exporter does not change the exposure, and origin misdeclaration is fraud, not a workaround.

## 4. Documentation Discipline and Landed Cost Modeling

Turn customs from a surprise into a process:

- **Written classification.** Maintain an internal classification memo per SKU: HTS number, reasoning, and supporting product data. Where value is high or interpretation is genuinely uncertain, a binding ruling from CBP (through the eRuling process) locks the classification.
- **Supplier cooperation clause.** The supply contract should require the manufacturer to provide accurate product descriptions, material declarations and certificates of origin — and to notify changes in substrate or coating that could affect classification.
- **Broker alignment.** Review your customs broker's entries annually against your classification memos; classification drift between SKUs is common and quietly expensive.
- **Landed cost model.** Build the program P&L on landed cost: factory price + freight + insurance + MFN duty + current Section/reciprocal tariffs + a trade-remedy risk buffer. Update tariff layers quarterly; repricing conversations go far better when the duty component is a visible, explained line.
- **First-sale and valuation awareness.** For multi-tier transactions, agree valuation structure with your broker before the first entry — adjustments after entry invite audits.

## FAQ

### Which HTS heading covers paper baking cups and muffin liners?

Most paper muffin cups, cake cups and baking liners classify under heading 4823 as paper articles not elsewhere specified, while coated baking paper generally falls under heading 4811. The correct 10-digit subheading depends on the specific material, coating and form — classification should be confirmed with product data and, where significant, a CBP binding ruling rather than copied from another importer's paperwork.

### Do Section 301 tariffs apply to baking cups from China?

They have applied to various paper and packaging product classifications from China, at rates that have changed multiple times. The correct answer depends on the exact HTS code, the current tariff action in force on the entry date, and any exclusions. Check the current Federal Register notices and CBP guidance at entry time, and model the cost at the current rate rather than quoting historical figures.

### How does an anti-dumping investigation affect orders already in production?

If a petition is filed and the Department of Commerce initiates an investigation, entries on or after the initiation date can become subject to retroactive cash deposits or assessments if the case ends in an affirmative determination. Goods in production or on the water at initiation carry this exposure — which is why importers track pending petitions in their product categories rather than waiting for the final order.

### Can I avoid trade remedy duties by shipping through another country?

No. AD/CVD duties follow the country of manufacture and the product scope. Transshipment or misdeclaring origin to evade trade remedies is customs fraud, exposes the importer of record to penalties and back duties, and is treated as such by CBP. Legitimate options exist: source from a country outside the order's scope, or apply for a scope ruling if you believe your product falls outside it.
