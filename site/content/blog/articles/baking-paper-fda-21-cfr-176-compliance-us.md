## Introduction

For any baking paper destined for the United States market, FDA compliance is the entry ticket. US buyers — from food manufacturers to distributors and retail private-label programs — will typically ask for evidence that the paper complies with Title 21 of the Code of Federal Regulations, and more specifically with Part 176, which governs paper and paperboard components intended for food contact. Yet requests often stop at a vague "FDA approved?" question, and suppliers sometimes respond with marketing claims rather than regulation-specific documentation. Both sides can do better.

This guide explains what 21 CFR Part 176 actually covers for baking paper, how the aqueous and fatty food provisions differ, what a Letter of Guarantee should say, which specification documents buyers should collect before approving a supplier, and the common gaps that surface during customer audits.

## What 21 CFR Part 176 Covers

Part 176 is the section of the FDA's food additive regulations that addresses "indirect food additives: paper and paperboard components." It sets conditions under which substances used in papermaking — pulps, sizing agents, wet-strength resins, defoamers, coatings, and processing aids — may be used in paper and paperboard that contacts food. For baking paper, the two most relevant sections are:

- 21 CFR 176.170 — Components of paper and paperboard in contact with aqueous and fatty foods. This is the key provision for baking paper, because butter, cake batters, and cookie doughs are fatty or aqueous foods. The section lists permitted substances and imposes extractives limits: the finished paper must not yield specified migrants into the food simulants above set thresholds, tested under defined conditions of use.
- 21 CFR 176.180 — Components of paper and paperboard in contact with dry food. Relevant for paper used with dry baked goods in limited-contact scenarios, with fewer restrictions than 176.170.

Other sections buyers may encounter include 176.260 (paperboard used in contact with dry food) and 176.300 (papers used in filtration), but for baking parchment and baking cups sold for direct food contact in US kitchens, 176.170 is the operative standard. Note the regulatory logic: FDA does not "approve" finished baking paper. Compliance means the paper's components are used consistent with the listings and limitations in the regulation, and the finished article meets any applicable extractives limits.

## Conditions of Use: Why Temperature Matters

Within 176.170, permitted substances and limits are tied to conditions of use — descriptions of how hot and how long the food contact occurs. High-temperature contact, such as baking at oven temperatures, is the most demanding condition and narrows the set of compliant additives. This matters for buyers because a paper compliant for wrapping sandwiches at room temperature is not automatically compliant for lining a 200°C baking tray. When you collect compliance documentation, check that the stated conditions of use actually include high-temperature baking contact, not just ambient or refrigerated contact.

## Letters of Guarantee: What They Should Say

A Letter of Guarantee (LOG) is the supplier's written assurance that its product complies with specified FDA provisions. US buyers and their customers rely on LOGs to document due diligence. A weak LOG creates more risk than no LOG at all, because it documents compliance without actually describing it. A useful LOG for baking paper should:

- Identify the product precisely — product name, grade, and specification number, not just "baking paper."
- Cite the specific regulatory sections: 21 CFR 176.170 and/or 176.180, plus any other applicable provisions (for example, substances covered elsewhere in Parts 175–178 if coatings are used).
- State the applicable conditions of use, including high-temperature baking.
- State the food types covered (aqueous, fatty, dry).
- Identify the legal entity issuing the guarantee and be signed by an authorized representative.
- Reference the specification revision and date, so the LOG can be tied to change-control records.

Buyers should also understand the chain of responsibility: the paper mill or converter issues the LOG to its customer, and each party in the import chain typically relies on and re-issues guarantees downstream. If you import directly, be prepared to provide your own LOG to your US customers, supported by your supplier's documentation.

## Specification Documentation Buyers Should Collect

Before approving a baking paper supplier for the US market, collect and file:

1. A formal product specification sheet: grammage, silicone coating type and weight, dimensions, and food-contact declaration.
2. The FDA compliance statement or LOG citing 21 CFR 176.170/176.180 with conditions of use.
3. Extractives or migration test data referenced to the regulation's simulants and time-temperature conditions, from an accredited laboratory.
4. Declarations for ancillary components: silicone coating raw materials, release agents, and any wet-strength or sizing additives, ideally with their own regulatory references.
5. If a substance falls outside a listed use, documentation of an appropriate FDA Food Contact Notification (FCN) or other regulatory basis, including the FCN's scope of use — FCNs are limited to the notifier's specific product and supplier.
6. Change-notification commitments, so you learn when formulations or coating suppliers change.

## Common Compliance Gaps in Practice

Audits of baking paper supply chains repeatedly surface the same gaps. Watch for:

- Generic LOGs that cite "FDA compliant" without naming 176.170, the conditions of use, or the specific product — unverifiable and effectively worthless.
- High-temperature baking paper documented only for ambient contact conditions.
- Test reports with no traceable link to the specification revision being shipped.
- Silicone coating systems whose raw material compliance is asserted but never evidenced.
- Expired or superseded documents still circulating in buyer files after a formulation change.
- Confusion between FDA compliance and third-party certifications — a BRCGS or ISO certificate audits production systems; it does not demonstrate 21 CFR 176 substance compliance.

## FAQ

### Q: Does FDA approve baking paper?

A: No. FDA does not approve or certify finished paper products. Compliance with 21 CFR Part 176 means the components used in the paper are consistent with the regulation's listings and limitations, and the finished paper meets applicable extractives limits. Documentation, not an approval seal, is the evidence.

### Q: Is 21 CFR 176.180 enough for baking paper used with cookies and pastries?

A: It depends on the food. 176.180 covers dry food contact. Butter-rich doughs, batters, and oily pastries are fatty or aqueous foods, which brings 176.170 into play. For most baking paper applications, buyers should require documentation under 176.170, including the high-temperature conditions of use.

### Q: What is a Letter of Guarantee and who issues it?

A: It is a written assurance from the supplier — typically the mill or converter — that the product complies with the specific FDA provisions cited. US buyers often pass equivalent guarantees to their own customers. A valid LOG names the product, cites the regulation sections, states conditions of use and food types, and is signed by an authorized representative.

### Q: What documents should I request before first shipment?

A: At minimum: a product specification sheet, an LOG citing 21 CFR 176.170/176.180 with conditions of use, accredited-laboratory extractives or migration data tied to that specification, coating and additive declarations, and change-notification terms. Keep them tied to specification revisions so audits can trace shipments to evidence.

## Conclusion

FDA 21 CFR 176 compliance for baking paper is not a stamp — it is a documented chain from raw material listings through finished-paper extractives limits to a Letter of Guarantee that actually describes the product and its conditions of use. Buyers who specify 176.170 for fatty and high-temperature contact, collect traceable test data, and manage document versions will pass customer audits with far less friction than those who settle for a one-line "FDA approved" statement. In the US market, the quality of your compliance file is part of your product.
