## Introduction

Interfolded dry wax deli paper looks like one product on a distributor's price list, but the specification that decides whether it works in your operation is the base-paper weight — and for the two weights most commonly quoted, 23 gsm and 30 gsm, the difference is felt in the hand, in the dispenser and in the cost per sheet rather than on the data sheet. Choosing between them is not a matter of picking the heavier number by default: the lighter sheet can be the better commercial choice in dry, high-volume, single-sheet service, while the heavier one earns its cost where handling stiffness and wrap integrity matter. This guide works through the decision in the order a buyer meets it: what the two weights describe, where each fits, and how packing, storage and reorder terms should be locked down before the first carton ships. For the paper side of these programmes, buyers can review our [silicone-coated parchment sheets and rolls](/products/baking-parchment-paper/) specifications and sample terms.

## 1. What 23 GSM and 30 GSM Actually Describe

Grammage is the mass of paper per square metre, and in this category suppliers may report it on the base sheet or the finished sheet — the first thing to confirm in writing, because two quotes reading "23 gsm" may not compare the same sheet. The dry wax construction is the other half of the specification: the wax treatment is designed so the sheet stays relatively dry-feeling and non-slick in the hand, pulling cleanly from an interfolded dispenser without the cling of some coated formats. Interfolding — sheets folded over each other so each pull presents the next — is common to both weights, so the base weight is the variable the decision turns on. Both weights are offered in the same product family: our [interfolded dry wax deli paper sheets](/products/interfolded-deli-paper/) are quoted with two base-paper weights in the standard 500-sheet box format — a real sourcing choice, not a catalogue abstraction.

## 2. Where the Lighter 23 GSM Weight Fits

The lighter construction is built for volume. In dry, short-contact service — lining bread and pastry baskets, interleaving between stacked items, single-sheet wrap for sandwiches that go straight into a bag, or counter sheets pulled one at a time at high speed — a 23 gsm sheet does the job at the lowest cost per sheet, and its flexibility feeds more forgivingly through pop-up dispensers. It is also the natural choice where sheets are discarded quickly and cost exposure is counted in tens of thousands of pulls per week. The trade-off is stiffness and substance: a lighter sheet carries less body, so a tight wrap around a wet or heavy item takes more operator technique, and there is less barrier margin if the food load is greasier than the specification was written for. That is the failure distributors see in the field: a spec written for a dry sandwich programme gets reassigned by a store manager to a wet burger line it was never approved for. The fix is procedural: write the intended food load into the specification and confirm the weight against that load during sampling, not after the first pallet lands. ## 3. Where the Heavier 30 GSM Weight Fits

The heavier construction buys handling substance. Where the sheet has to hold a fold around a loaded sandwich, line a tray carried across the counter, or present a basket customers lift and inspect, the additional body of a 30 gsm base reads immediately as a crisper fold and a more substantial feel — which is why front-of-house presentation programmes usually justify the heavier weight. It also widens the margin on wetter, oilier loads: more fibre mass means more capacity before the sheet approaches what its wax treatment was specified to handle, and that margin keeps a programme stable as menu items drift between stores. The costs are equally concrete: a higher price per sheet, more freight weight per carton, and a slightly firmer feed behaviour the pack specification has to account for — none of which disqualify the heavier sheet, they simply mean the decision follows the application, not a default. Operations running both weights should keep them as separate line items, because the most common field failure here is a purchasing consolidation that quietly merges two approved constructions into one. ## 4. Packing, Dispenser Fit and Reorder Terms

The base-weight choice interacts with the pack more than buyers expect. Interfolded sheets are packed in fixed counts per box — the standard 500-sheet dispenser box on this programme — and the box has to feed the counter's dispenser, so sheet stiffness, fold geometry and pack height belong in the specification even though they look like packing details. Heavier sheets fill a box faster in caliper terms, so count, pack height and dispenser compatibility should be confirmed together, not assumed from a previous order. Storage adds the same logic: waxed paper is still paper, and stockroom humidity affects feel and feed, so cartons should be stored sealed and rotated like any food-contact consumable. On the commercial side, MOQ and terms depend on the sheet size, the base weight, the pack format and the confirmed specification — a supplier who asks for those details before quoting is protecting the timeline, not delaying it. Treat the first approved order as the reference standard: retain a sealed sample of each approved weight, reference the base weight, dimensions and pack count in every purchase order, and require food-contact documentation for the shipped grade against the destination-market benchmarks — FDA 21 CFR Part 176, LFGB/BfR Recommendation XXXVI or EU Regulation (EC) No 1935/2004 — with declarations per shipment. ## FAQ

### Is 30 gsm always better than 23 gsm?

No. The heavier sheet gives more stiffness and more margin on wetter loads, but for dry, single-sheet, high-volume service the lighter weight usually delivers the same outcome at a lower cost per sheet. The right weight follows the food load and the handling, not the bigger number.

### Which weight should a mixed deli and bakery operation choose?

Most mixed operations run both: the lighter weight for dry, high-volume single-sheet service, the heavier one for wrap-and-carry and presentation baskets — kept as separate specifications in purchasing so an approved construction is never silently replaced by the other.

### Does the base weight change how the sheets feed in a dispenser box?

It can. Heavier sheets are firmer in feed, so pack height, fold geometry and dispenser compatibility should be confirmed with the actual box and dispenser during sampling — feed behaviour belongs in the trial, not the assumption.

### What should I confirm in writing before ordering?

Four things: whether the quoted gsm refers to the base paper or the finished sheet, the sheet dimensions, the pack count and box format, and the food-contact documentation for the exact construction and destination market. MOQ and terms depend on size, pack format and the confirmed specification, so quote against the complete spec.

## Conclusion

The 23 gsm vs 30 gsm decision on interfolded dry wax deli paper is an application decision, not a quality ranking: the lighter weight wins on cost per sheet and feed forgiveness in dry, single-sheet, high-volume service, while the heavier weight earns its premium where fold stiffness, presentation and margin on wetter loads matter. Buyers who write the intended food load, the quoted-gsm basis, the pack format and the documentation requirements into the specification — and confirm them with physical samples in the real dispenser and the real menu item — get a programme that holds across stores, reorders and menu changes.
