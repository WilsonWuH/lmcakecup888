## Introduction

Custom logo parchment paper sheets raise three specification questions before any quote is meaningful: which sheet sizes fit the pans, trays and baskets in daily use; where the logo can print so it stays visible after product sits on it; and what a physical sample must prove before the first production run is approved. ## 1. Sheet Size: Start From the Pan, the Tray and the Application

The right sheet size is not a design decision — it is an operations decision, and it starts with an inventory of what the sheets must serve. A bakery lining full sheet pans needs a different format than a cafe lining toast baskets, a deli interleaving stacked portions, or a brand packing cookies into retail boxes, and most operations use more than one format in the same week. The practical route is to measure the actual equipment — pan dimensions, tray footprint, basket mouth, the product it must wrap or separate — and then match against the supplier's standard sheet formats first, because standard sizes avoid die-cut tooling and usually shorten the path to a first sample. Custom die-cut sizes are entirely feasible for programmes where the application demands them, but they add a cutting specification to confirm: nominal dimensions, corner shape and tolerance band all belong in the specification, not in assumptions. MOQ and commercial terms depend on the size, color count, print coverage, packaging and confirmed specification of the job.

## 2. Print Placement: Where a Logo Works on a Working Sheet

Print placement on a parchment sheet is governed by what the sheet does after it is printed. A sheet that lines a tray or basket shows only its edges once product is on it, so a repeating band printed along the edge — or the sheet border on a basket liner — is the placement that stays visible in use, while a logo printed in the centre is largely hidden under the food and spends ink on area nobody sees. A sheet used as an interleave or a wrap is judged differently: there the logo appears on the fold and on the surface facing the customer, so placement follows how the sheet is folded or dispensed rather than how it lies flat. Placement also interacts with cut lines: on sheets trimmed from a printed web, the artwork must be positioned so the logo survives the trim on every cut, which is why placement should be agreed on a layout drawing that shows the print relative to the trim, not on a loose logo file. A restrained one-color mark in the correct zone routinely reads better than a large multi-color design in the wrong one, and it also simplifies press setup on every run. ## 3. The Sample Checklist: What a Logo Sheet Must Prove Before Approval

The sample round is the cheapest point in the programme to catch a specification error, and it works only if it is judged against a written checklist instead of a quick glance. The core items are straightforward. Size and format: measure the sample sheet against the confirmed nominal dimensions and confirm the die-cut shape, corner and trim behaviour on a full stack, not a single sheet. Print placement: hold the printed sheet in the actual application — on the pan, in the basket, folded as it will be used — and confirm the logo sits in the visible zone and survives the trim on every sheet. Colour and registration: compare the printed mark against the approved artwork under the lighting where the product is sold, and check edge sharpness on a stack to catch registration drift. Ink behaviour on the silicone surface: confirm the ink sits cleanly without offsetting onto adjacent sheets or onto the baked product, and that it holds up when the sheet is folded or lightly creased. Release under real use: bake the actual product on the sample sheet and confirm release and clean surface are unaffected by the printed area. Count and packing: confirm sheets per pack and the packing format against the order specification. A sample produced on the same construction as the production run — same base weight, same coating, same print method — is the only kind that can answer these questions, so the checklist should state that requirement explicitly. ## FAQ

### What sheet sizes can be ordered as custom logo parchment paper sheets?

Both standard sheet formats and custom die-cut sizes are possible, and the right starting point is the equipment the sheets must serve — pans, trays, baskets or retail packs. Standard sizes usually avoid die-cut tooling and reach a sample faster, while custom sizes add a cutting specification (nominal dimensions, corner shape, tolerance) to confirm. MOQ depends on the size, color count, print coverage, packaging and confirmed specification.

### Where should the logo be placed on a printed parchment sheet?

In the zone that stays visible in actual use. On tray and basket liners that is the edge or border, since product covers the centre; on interleaves and wraps it is the surface that faces the customer after folding. Placement should be locked on a layout drawing showing the print relative to the trim lines, so the logo survives on every cut sheet.

### Can a small-batch order carry a multi-color logo?

Yes, multi-color printing is available, but each additional colour adds plate and press setup to the job, and that setup is largely fixed regardless of quantity — so on small batches a one-color mark is usually the economical choice. Colour count should be decided together with sheet size and coverage, because all three shape the quote and the MOQ position.

### What should a printed parchment sample be judged on?

Size and trim accuracy, logo placement in the visible zone, colour match and registration against approved artwork, ink adhesion and offset behaviour on the silicone surface, release on the actual baked product, and count and packing per the order specification. The sample must be produced on the same construction as the production run for the results to carry over.
