## Introduction

Canadian buyers sit between two well-documented regimes they often know by heart — the US FDA system and the EU framework — and a Canadian system that works differently from both. There is no Health Canada certificate to file away, no pre-market notification number to quote, and no single positive list titled for packaging paper. That absence of paperwork is not an absence of obligation. Under the Food and Drug Regulations, a package that transfers a harmful substance to food makes the food itself illegal to sell, and the importer carries the consequences.

This guide explains how food contact paper compliance actually works in Canada: the legal standard, the documents Health Canada and CFIA expect to see, the importer duties under the Safe Food for Canadians Regulations, and the labeling rules that apply when paper packaging reaches consumers. It closes with a verification checklist wholesale buyers can send to any supplier today.

## 1. The Legal Standard: Division 23 of the Food and Drug Regulations

Canada's core requirement lives in Division 23 of the Food and Drug Regulations (SOR/2016-193), section B.23.001. The rule is simple in form and demanding in effect: no person shall sell food in a package that may impart to the food any substance that could be harmful to the consumer. There is no moveable list of approved papers attached to the section — the standard is outcome-based, and the burden of demonstrating safety rests on the party placing the food on the market.

Two consequences follow for packaging buyers. First, unlike the US system, Health Canada does not issue food contact notifications or approvals for packaging materials; there is no regulatory document that says "this parchment is approved." Second, compliance in Canada is demonstrated through evidence — composition disclosure, formulation review against recognized references, and migration or toxicological reasoning — rather than through a certificate. Health Canada's published guidance on packaging materials, including its guidelines for determining the acceptability of food packaging substances, describes exactly this process: evaluate the formulation against recognized references such as the US FDA's 21 CFR 176 for paper components, and confirm that migration to food is negligible under realistic conditions of use.

For buyers of baking paper and baking cups this is good news in practice: a paper that complies with FDA 21 CFR 176 and carries supporting formulation data is usually straightforward to justify for Canada. But "usually" is doing real work — the justification must exist on paper, in the buyer's file, before the first shipment clears.

## 2. The Letter of Guarantee: Canada's Workhorse Document

Because there is no approval to point to, Canadian commercial practice runs on the Letter of Guarantee (LOG). The LOG is a signed statement from the packaging supplier attesting that the material, as specified, complies with the Food and Drugs Act and Regulations when used under defined conditions. It is the document food manufacturers attach to their supplier approval files and the first thing a CFIA inspector or a retail customer's compliance team asks for.

A LOG that protects a buyer contains more than a compliance sentence. It should name the exact legal entity issuing it, identify the product by specification number or grade, state the conditions of use it covers (direct food contact, temperatures up to a stated baking temperature, fat contact), and be dated and signed by an accountable officer. Generic LOGs that "cover all products" are worth little; when a complaint or inspection arrives, specificity is what converts a document into a defense. Buyers should also confirm the LOG issuer matches the paper manufacturer, not just an intermediary — a point developed in the sourcing context below.

## 3. Importer Duties Under the Safe Food for Canadians Regulations

The Safe Food for Canadians Act and its Regulations (SFCR) shifted Canadian food safety to a preventive-controls model, and packaging sits inside that system. Importers of food must hold a licence, implement preventive controls that address hazards associated with the food — including hazards arising from its packaging — and maintain traceability records one step back and one step forward.

For packaging buyers this translates into three file requirements. First, supplier approval: the packaging supplier should be evaluated and documented, with the LOG, formulation data and any third-party certification (BRCGS, FSSC 22000) on record. Second, traceability: each packaging lot should be identifiable to a shipment, so a retention sample and lot number system is not optional. Third, verification: periodic re-testing or re-confirmation that the material still matches the specification, particularly after any supplier change of coating, furnish or production site. Retail and foodservice customers increasingly audit these files directly, and an importer who cannot produce them within a day looks uncontrolled regardless of how good the paper is.

## 4. Labeling: Bilingual Rules and Industrial Packaging Distinctions

When paper packaging reaches the retail consumer packaged together with food — retail packs of baking cups, boxed parchment, individual foodservice portions — Canadian labeling law applies. The Consumer Packaging and Labelling Act requires prepackaged consumer products to carry mandatory information in both English and French, including the product identity and the dealer name and address, with quantity declarations per the Regulations. For baking cups sold at retail, that means the pack itself, not only the food inside, must satisfy bilingual requirements.

Industrial packaging follows different logic: a master carton of 10,000 baking cups destined for a commercial bakery is not a prepackaged consumer product, and the CPLA's bilingual requirements generally do not attach to it. Buyers should still require carton marking that carries lot numbers, product specification and manufacturing dates for traceability — the SFCR file depends on it. The distinction to hold on to is this: compliance obligations follow the point of sale, and the same paper can require different labeling in retail and industrial channels.

## 5. Emerging Scrutiny: PFAS, Mineral Oil and Forward Compliance

Canadian enforcement attention on food packaging is rising along with international trends. PFAS in food packaging has drawn petitions and policy attention federally, and Health Canada's broader PFAS risk-management work signals that greaseproof papers relying on fluorinated treatments face a shrinking future. Buyers writing five-year supply agreements should already require PFAS-free evidence — total organic fluorine screening or equivalent — for grease-resistant grades. Mineral oil and heavy metals in paper, already regulated topics in the EU, appear in retailer specifications across Canada even where no national limit exists, because national retail chains harmonize to their strictest market.

The practical takeaway: Canadian compliance today is formulation-driven and evidence-driven. Buyers who build a file once — LOG, formulation disclosure, test reports, certification scope, traceability records — can re-verify it in hours at every renewal, and answer customer audits without a scramble.

## FAQ

### Does Health Canada approve or certify food contact paper packaging?

No. Health Canada does not issue approvals, notifications or certificates for food packaging materials. Compliance is demonstrated through evidence: formulation disclosure, evaluation against recognized references such as FDA 21 CFR 176 for paper components, and migration reasoning for the conditions of use. The supplier's Letter of Guarantee plus supporting data is the standard evidence package.

### What is a Letter of Guarantee and what should it contain?

A Letter of Guarantee is the supplier's signed attestation that the packaging material complies with the Food and Drugs Act and Regulations under stated conditions of use. A useful LOG names the issuing legal entity, identifies the product specification, states the covered conditions (direct contact, maximum temperature, fat contact), and is dated and signed by an accountable officer. Blanket LOGs covering "all products" should be rejected in favor of specification-specific documents.

### I already have FDA and EU compliance documents. Are they enough for Canada?

They are a strong foundation, not a substitute. FDA 21 CFR 176 conformity and EU Framework Regulation 1935/2004 evidence support the safety argument Canada's outcome-based standard requires, but the buyer still needs a Canada-addressed LOG, the SFCR importer file (supplier approval, traceability, verification records), and bilingual labeling for any retail-bound packs.

## Conclusion

Canada asks a different question than the US or EU — not "who approved this?" but "can you show it is safe?" Buyers who understand that the Food and Drug Regulations' Division 23 standard is evidence-based, who hold a specification-specific Letter of Guarantee, who maintain the preventive-controls file the SFCR expects, and who keep an eye on PFAS and mineral-oil scrutiny, end up with a compliance posture that satisfies inspectors, retail auditors and end customers alike. The document file is the product; the paper is only half of it.
